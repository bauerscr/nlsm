\section{Block spin transforms}
\label{sec:block-gauss}

This explains roughly \cite[Section~3.2]{OpenAI-O3}. See also \cite{MR588686}.

The lattice $\Lambda$ has positively oriented edge set $E$ and blocks $B$.
Fields on the lattice are denoted $\phi$ and block spin fields $V$.
Let $d:\R^\Lambda\to\R^E$ be the discrete gradient and $d^*:\R^E\to\R^\Lambda$ its adjoint.



\subsection{Nonlinear block spin transforms}

The construction uses `soft' block spin transforms defined in terms of a penalization parameter $a>0$
(which will actually be set to $a=1$).
For a block $Y \subset \Lambda$, the linear block average of $\phi: \Lambda \to \R$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
 (Q\phi)_Y=\sum_{x\in Y}w_Y(x)\phi_x,
 \qquad w_Y(x)\ge0,\qquad \sum_{x\in Y}w_Y(x)=1.
\end{equation}
The nonlinear block spin average $\widehat m_Y(q)$ of a spin configuration $q: \Lambda \to S^2$ is then defined by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hatm}
  m_Y(q) = (Qq)_Y, \qquad \widehat m_Y(q) =
  \begin{cases}
      \frac{m_Y(q)}{|m_Y(q)|} & (|m_Y(q)|\geq \frac12)
      \\
      q_{\tau_Y} & (|m_Y(q)| < \frac12),
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_Y$ is a random point in $Y$ chosen according to distribution (...).
The block spin transform of a measure on configurations $q: \Lambda \to S^2$ is done by inserting the following reblocking kernel into the measure for $q$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:nl-block-average}
\mathcal B(q, dV)
\propto \prod_{Y \in \Lambda'} e^{-\frac{a}{2}|V_Y-\widehat m_Y(q)|^2} \, d\omega(V_Y)
\end{equation}
and integrating over $q$. This defines a new measure on block configurations $V :\Lambda' \to S^2$ where $Y\in\Lambda'$ are the blocks of the original lattice $\Lambda$.


\subsection{Gaussian block spin transforms}

For Gaussian measures and using the linear block average $Q\phi$ is instead of $\widehat m$,
the block spin transform is an explicit recursion for the kinetic term. This recursion was analyzed in
\cite{MR588686} (with hard rather than soft average).

For the initial Gaussian measure, the kinetic term is given by the Laplacian on $\Lambda$ \cite[above (3.4)]{OpenAI-O3}:
\begin{equation}
  K_{\varnothing} = d^* d.
\end{equation}
Tthe next-scale kinetic term is defined by \cite[(3.4)]{OpenAI-O3}:
\begin{equation}
K_{h+1} = a\Id-a^2Q(K_h+aQ^*Q)^{-1}Q^*.
\end{equation}
This choice is explained by the following well-known fact.

\begin{proposition}
For a given block spin field $V\in\R^B$, 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Gauss-var}
 \frac12\langle V,K_{h+1}V\rangle
 =\min_{\phi\in\R^\Lambda} \mathcal E_V(\phi)
 =\min_{\phi\in\R^\Lambda}
 \left\{\frac12\langle\phi,K_h\phi\rangle
              +\frac a2\|V-Q\phi\|^2\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here $\Lambda$ is the set of fine sites and $B$ the number
of blocks.  The minimization is over unrestricted real fields.
%
In particular, for any $V \in \R^B$ with $\phi_V$ the minimizer of $\mathcal E_V$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi)
  &= \min \mathcal E_V + \frac12 \langle \phi-\phi_V, \operatorname{Hess} \mathcal E_V(0)(\phi-\phi_V)\rangle 
  \\
  &= \frac12 \langle V, K_{h+1} V\rangle + \frac12 \langle \phi-\phi_V, \operatorname{Hess} \mathcal E_V(0)(\phi-\phi_V)\rangle 
  .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Set $A=K_h+aQ^*Q$. For $a>0$, this matrix is positive definite whenever $\ker K_h\cap\ker Q=\{0\}$.
The positive gradient term in $K_h$ makes its kernel consist of constants, while
$Q$ preserves constants, so this condition holds.
Expanding the energy gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_V(\phi)
 =\frac12\langle\phi,A\phi\rangle
  -a\langle\phi,Q^*V\rangle+\frac a2\|V\|^2.
\end{equation}
Its unique minimizer therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}
 A\phi_V=aQ^*V,\qquad
 \phi_V=a(K_h+aQ^*Q)^{-1}Q^*V.
\end{equation}
Completing the square yields
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_V(\phi)
 =\frac12\langle\phi-\phi_V,A(\phi-\phi_V)\rangle
  +\frac12\langle V,(a\Id-a^2QA^{-1}Q^*)V\rangle.
\end{equation}
The first term vanishes at $\phi=\phi_V$, giving the stated formula
for $K_{h+1}$.
\end{proof}


Note that $K_h$ is not finite range when $h \neq \varnothing$.
A sequence of block spin transformations is called \emph{history}
and the corresponding kinetic term $K_h$.


\subsection{Covariance estimates}
\label{subsec:free-covariance-estimates}

For the estimates, we use square blocks of side $L$ on
$\mathbb Z^2$ and a fixed smooth averaging profile $\chi$, as in
\cite[Sections~3.1 and~4.1]{OpenAI-O3}.
Fix $0<\rho<1/4$ and a nonzero, nonnegative, even function
$\chi\in C_c^\infty((-\rho,\rho))$, independently of $L$ and
of the history.  If $c_Y$ is the centre of $Y$, set
\begin{equation}
 w_Y(x)=
 \frac{\chi((x_1-c_{Y,1})/L)\chi((x_2-c_{Y,2})/L)}
 {\displaystyle\sum_{z\in Y}
   \chi((z_1-c_{Y,1})/L)\chi((z_2-c_{Y,2})/L)},
 \qquad x\in Y.
 \label{eq:smooth-block-weights}
\end{equation}
Take $L$ to be a sufficiently large integer. The normalization denominator satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{z\in Y}
 \chi\!\left(\frac{z_1-c_{Y,1}}L\right)
 \chi\!\left(\frac{z_2-c_{Y,2}}L\right)
 =L^2\left(\int_{\mathbb R}\chi(t)\,dt\right)^2+o(L^2)
 \qquad (L\to\infty).
 \label{eq:block-weight-normalization}
\end{equation}
The weights have zero centred first moment,
$\max_x w_Y(x)\le c_\chi L^{-2}$, and their discrete derivatives
of order $k$ have $\ell^1$ norm at most $c_{\chi,k}L^{-k}$.
The constants do not depend on $L$ or the history (number of steps).

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:uniform-covariance-estimates}
Fix $a>0$.
Let $K=K_h$ be the bulk kinetic term obtained from $d^*d$ by
any finite history of square block transformations
with the same weights \eqref{eq:smooth-block-weights}.
Every transformation in the history uses the same block side $L$,
measured in the lattice units of that step.
Set
\begin{equation}
 C=(K+aQ^*Q)^{-1},\qquad
 \overline C_L=\frac1{L^2}\sum_{x\in Y_0}C_{xx},
 \label{eq:free-fluctuation-covariance}
\end{equation}
where $Y_0$ is a reference block and $w_x=w_{Y_0}(x)$.
There is a constant $M$, independent of $L$ and $h$, such that,
for all $z,r,s\in\mathbb Z^2$,
\begin{align}
 \left|\overline C_L-\frac{\log L}{2\pi}\right|&\leq M,\\
 \frac1{L^2}\sum_{\substack{x\in Y_0\\y\in\mathbb Z^2}}
   |K_{xy}|\,|C_{xy}-C_{xx}|\,|x-y|^2&\leq M,\\
 \sum_{x,y\in Y_0}w_xw_y|C_{xy}|&\leq M.
 \label{eq:geom-uniform-bounds}
 \\
 \left|C_{z+r,z+s}-C_{z+r,z}-C_{z,z+s}+C_{zz}\right|
   &\le M|r|\,|s|.
 \label{eq:free-covariance-increments}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The uniform ellipticity, normalization $K_h(p)=|p|^2+O(|p|^4)$
and exponential kernel bounds of \cite[Section~4.2]{OpenAI-O3},
together with the rapid Fourier decay of the sampled weights,
give by the one-cell Fourier inversion formula
\begin{equation}
 C_{xy}=G_L(x-y)+R_{h,L}(x,y),\qquad
 G_L=(d^*d+L^{-2}\Id)^{-1},\qquad
 \sup_{h,L,x,y}|R_{h,L}(x,y)|<\infty.
 \label{eq:free-massive-comparison}
\end{equation}
Indeed, inversion of the rank-one observation term cancels the
principal Fourier pole, and after subtracting $G_L$ the remaining
alias sums are uniformly summable; the three estimates then follow from
$G_L(0)=(\log L)/(2\pi)+O(1)$,
$|G_L(r)-G_L(0)|\le c\log(2+|r|)$, and
$G_L\ge0$, $\sum_rG_L(r)=L^2$, using the exponential moments
of $K_h$ for the second estimate and $\max w_x\le c_\chi L^{-2}$
for the third.  The diagonal asymptotic follows by comparing the
Fourier integral of $G_L$ with the annular integral
\begin{equation}
 \int_{1/L\leq |p|\leq1}
       \frac{d^2p}{(2\pi)^2|p|^2}
   =\frac1{2\pi}\int_{1/L}^1\frac{dr}{r}
   =\frac{\log L}{2\pi}.
 \label{eq:geom-shell-log}
\end{equation}
The mass regularizes momenta below $L^{-1}$; that region and
the difference between the lattice integrand and the annular
integrand contribute only $O(1)$.  This comparison concerns
$G_L$, not an exact scalar Fourier representation of $C$,
which is only block-translation invariant.
Finally, uniform ellipticity and comparison with the massless
lattice Green function bound the variance of an increment
over $r$ by a constant times $|r|^2$; Cauchy--Schwarz then gives
\eqref{eq:free-covariance-increments}.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Lifting the energy}

\begin{proposition}
The renormalized kinetic term can be written as 
\begin{equation}
 K_h
 =d^* \mathsf A_h^* \mathsf A_h d
 = c_0d^*d+d^*\ell_h^*\ell_hd,
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\frac12\langle q,K_hq\rangle
=\frac{c_0}{2}\|dq\|^2+\frac12\|\ell_hdq\|^2,
\end{equation}
where the matrices $\ell_h$ and $\mathsf A_h$ are indexed by edges:
\begin{equation}
  \ell_h : \R^E \to \R^E,
  \qquad
  \mathsf A_h = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{c_0} I \\\ \ell_h \end{pmatrix} : \R^E \to \R^{E\times E}.
\end{equation}
In kernel notation, the edge operator acts by
\begin{equation}
 (\ell_h dq)_e=\sum_{f\in E}(\ell_h)_{ef}\,d_fq.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \cite[Section~4.2]{OpenAI-O3}, $cD\le K_h\le CD$ and $K_h(p)-D(p)=O(|p|^4)$ in Fourier space, where
$d(p)=\binom{e^{ip_1}-1}{e^{ip_2}-1}$ and $D=d^*d$.
There is $B_h^{\rm raw}=I+O(|p|^2)$ with $K_h=d^*B_h^{\rm raw}d$.
Writing $\mathsf c=(-d_2,d_1)$, we have $\mathsf c d=0$, so
\begin{equation}
 B_h=B_h^{\rm raw}+\lambda_0\mathsf c^*\mathsf c
 \quad\text{satisfies}\quad d^*B_hd=K_h.
\end{equation}
One fixed sufficiently large $\lambda_0$ makes $B_h$ uniformly
positive: near $p=0$ the raw lift is close to $I$; away from $p=0$,
ellipticity controls the longitudinal entry and the added term
controls the transverse entry, including the mixed term by a Schur
complement.  The bounds are uniform in the history.

To use the preprint's particular positive completion \cite[Section~4.3]{OpenAI-O3},
make the further modification
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde B_h^{-1}
 =B_h^{-1}+\lambda_1 B_h^{-1}\mathsf c^*\mathsf c B_h^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Since $\widetilde B_h^{-1}B_hd=d$, this still gives
$\widetilde B_hd=B_hd$ and $d^*\widetilde B_hd=K_h$.
Choose $c_0>0$ below the uniform lower bound of $\widetilde B_h$ and set
\begin{equation}
 \ell_h=(\widetilde B_h-c_0I)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Then $\mathsf A_h^*\mathsf A_h=c_0I+\ell_h^*\ell_h
=\widetilde B_h$, proving the decomposition and its quadratic-form
identity.  The uniform analyticity and strict positivity imply that
$\ell_h$ has an exponentially decaying kernel.  At empty history the
construction retains $\widetilde B_{\varnothing}=I$.
\end{proof}
There is redundancy between $c_0$ and $\ell_h$.
Initially, for $0<c_0<1$,
\begin{equation}
 K_{\varnothing}=d^*d,
 \qquad \ell_{\varnothing}=\sqrt{1-c_0}\,\Id.
\end{equation}
%
The constant $c_0>0$ is fixed (uniformly over histories) to reserve an explicit positive nearest-neighbour term for local energy estimates.
The operator $\ell_h$ has exponential tail.
Later $\ell_s$ is a spatially truncated version of $\ell_h$ used in the finite range integration.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:D0}
Define $D_0: \R^\Lambda \to \R^{E \times E \times B}$ by
\begin{equation}
  D_0
  =\begin{pmatrix}
       \sqrt{c_0}\,d\\ \ell_s d\\ -\sqrt a\,Q
  \end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}
       \mathsf A_s d\\ -\sqrt a\,Q
  \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \mathsf A_s=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{c_0}\Id\\\ell_s\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
%where
%\begin{equation}
% d,\ell_s d:\R^\Lambda\longrightarrow\R^E,
% \qquad Q:\R^\Lambda\longrightarrow\R^B.
%\end{equation}
Then the Hessian of the variational functional $\mathcal E_V(\phi)$ in \eqref{eq:Gauss-var} can be written as
\begin{equation}
 \|D_0\phi\|^2
 = \langle \phi, D_0^* D_0 \phi \rangle
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi)- \mathcal E_V(\phi_V)
  = \frac12 \|D_0\phi +c_V - s_V\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\phi_V$ is the minimizer of $\mathcal E_V$ and, for some $\nu_1,\nu_2,w$,
\begin{equation}
  c_V = \begin{pmatrix}
  0 \\ 0 \\ \sqrt{a}V
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  s_V
  = \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
  .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Hessian of the variational functional $\mathcal E_V(\phi)$ in \eqref{eq:Gauss-var} can be written as
\begin{equation}
 \|D_0\phi\|^2
 = \langle \phi, D_0^* D_0 \phi \rangle
 =c_0\sum_e|(d\phi)_e|^2
  +\sum_e|(\ell_s d\phi)_e|^2
  +a\sum_Y|(Q\phi)_Y|^2
\end{equation}
and the full Gaussian energy is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi) = \frac12 \|D_0\phi + c_V\|^2 ,
  \qquad
  c_V = \begin{pmatrix}
    0 \\ 0 \\ \sqrt{a} V 
  \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let $\phi_V = \operatorname{argmin} \mathcal E_V(\phi)$. Then 
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi_V + \eta) = \frac12 \|D_0\eta + s_V\|^2  = \frac12 \|D_0\eta\|^2 + \frac12 \|s_V\|^2,
  \qquad
  s_V
  = D_0\phi_V + c_V
  = \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
since $D^*s_V=0$. In other words,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi)- \mathcal E_V(\phi_V)
  = \frac12 \|D_0\phi - D_0\phi_V\|^2
  = \frac12 \|D_0\phi +c_V - s_V\|^2
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\section{Nonlinear background field coordinates}
\label{sec:background-field}

This roughly explains \cite[Section~5.2]{OpenAI-O3}.

\subsection{Fluctuation integral}

Throughout this section, $S\subset \Lambda$ are the nonfrozen fine sites (small field region) with
 one spin $q_x \in S^2$ integrated at each site $x\in S$. 
Their number is $n=|S|$.
For each site $x\in S$, a background field of reference spins $T_x\in  S^2$ is held fixed.
Consider the integral
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I=
 \int_{(S^2)^S} H(q)
    \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q)|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
    \prod_{x\in S}d\omega(q_x),
 \label{eq:spin-integral}
\end{equation}
where $|R(q)|^2$ is a reference energy and $d\omega$ the normalized measure on $S^2$.
% The function $H(q)$ is eventually used for nonlocal contributions not explicitly tracked in $|R(q)|^2$.


\subsection{Reference energy}

The precise form of the reference energy $|R(q)|^2$ is unimportant for the algebraic manipulations
in the subsequent subsections, but it motivates their choice.

$|R(q)|^2$ is a nonlinear version of the Gaussian energy with soft block spin conditioning,
defined in Section~\ref{sec:block-gauss}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi) - \mathcal E_V(\phi_V) = \frac12 \|D_0\phi+c_V-s_V\|^2.
\end{equation}
More precisely, first restrict $D_0: \R^{n} \to \R^{E \times E \times B}$ 
to nonfrozen sites $S \subset \Lambda$ and `selected' components of the range $R\subset E\times E \times B$
(selecting small gradients or so):
\begin{equation}
D = D_0|_{S\times R}.
\end{equation}
%
For the purpose of the discussion, we ignore this restriction; it does not change much.
Thus, up to this dropping rows and columns,
in Proposition~\ref{prop:D0}, it was shown that
\begin{equation}
D\phi +c_V -s_V = \begin{pmatrix}
  \sqrt{c_0} d\phi\\
  \ell_s d\phi\\
  \sqrt{a}V -\sqrt{a}Q\phi
\end{pmatrix}
-s_V,
  \qquad
  s_V = \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
.
\end{equation}
%
For $q: \R^\Lambda  \to S^2$, define $R(q)$ as a nonlinear version of this:
\begin{equation}
  R(q) = \begin{pmatrix}
  \sqrt{c_0} dq\\
  \ell_s dq\\
  \sqrt{a}(V-\widehat m(q))
  \end{pmatrix}
  - \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where we recall that $\widehat m_Y(q)$ is the nonlinear block average \eqref{eq:hatm}.

Concretely, the quantity $\tfrac12|R(q)|^2$ arises as follows.
The renormalized density representation \eqref{eq:density} describes the full density before or after that
step.  The connection is obtained by extracting the coarse kinetic
energy from the fine kinetic energy plus the block-spin observation
term.  Write \eqref{eq:density} as
\begin{equation}
 \rho_j(q)=e^{\mathfrak v_j|\Lambda_j|}
          e^{-b\mathcal E_{h,t}(q)}G_j(q).
\end{equation}
The block transformation integrates this against the block spin
kernel whose exponential part is
\begin{equation}
 e^{-\frac{ba}{2}\|V-\widehat m(q)\|^2}.
\end{equation}
Then the leading part of the energy decomposition has the following form:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cEqV}
 \mathcal E_{h,t}(q)
 +\frac a2\|V-\widehat m(q)\|^2
 =\mathcal E_{h',t'}(V)
  +\frac12|R(q;V)|^2+\mathcal L(q,V).
\end{equation}
Here $h',t'$ are the next-scale history and large field scale, $R(q;V)$
denotes the selected residual rows defined above and $\mathcal L(q,V)$ collects the remainder.

\subsection{Intrinsic tangent coordinates}

Consider a single site and reference spin $T \in S^2$.
For $u\in T^\perp$,
\begin{equation}
 \Exp_T u=\cos|u|\,T+\frac{\sin|u|}{|u|}u,
 \qquad \Exp_T0=T.
\end{equation}
The restriction $|u|<\pi$ parametrizes $S^2\setminus\{-T\}$ and the omitted point has area zero.
Note
\begin{equation}
 d\omega(\Exp_Tu)=J(u)\,d^2u,
 \qquad J(u)=\frac1{4\pi}\frac{\sin|u|}{|u|},
 \qquad J(0)=\frac1{4\pi}.
 \label{eq:area}
\end{equation}
In tangent coordinates, 
$q(u)=(\Exp_{T_x}u_x)_{x\in S}$ and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal U=\prod_{x\in S}
       \{u_x\in T_x^\perp:|u_x|<\pi\}.
\end{equation}
The spherical area formula gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I=
 \int_{\mathcal U} H(q(u))
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q(u))|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \prod_{x\in S}\bigl[J(u_x)\,d^2u_x\bigr].
\end{equation}
Here $d^2u_x$ is Euclidean area measure on $T_x^\perp$.
This integral has $2n$ real coordinates.

\subsection{Auxiliary normal coordinate}

Introduce one real variable $v_x\in\R$ at each nonfrozen site.
Since $A=D^*D$ is positive definite, the elementary
Gaussian integral gives
\begin{equation}
 1=\frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
       \int_{\R^n}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{v^*Av}{2g^2}\right\}d^nv.
 \label{eq:scalar-one}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the inserted probability law is a centered scalar
Gaussian with covariance $g^2A^{-1}$.  Its energy is
$v^*Av=|Dv|^2$.  No cutoff is imposed on $v$, and
the inserted variables are initially independent of the spin variables.
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal I={}&\frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 \int_{\R^n}\int_{\mathcal U} H(q(u))\\
 &\quad\times
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q(u))|^2+|Dv|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \prod_{x\in S}\bigl[J(u_x)\,d^2u_x\, dv_x\bigr].
 \label{eq:uv-integral}
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
There are now $2n+n=3n$ real integration coordinates.
The purpose of the extra coordinate is
to complete each tangent plane to the ambient space via
\begin{equation}
\xi_x=u_x+T_xv_x
.
\end{equation}
Thus the scalar $v_x$ is an auxiliary normal coordinate, not a new physical spin component.

\subsection{Combining the coordinates into an ambient field}

Conversely, for $\xi=(\xi_x)_{x\in S}\in(\R^3)^S$, define the tangential and normal directions
\begin{equation}
 u_x(\xi)=\xi_x-(T_x\cdot\xi_x)T_x,
 \qquad
 v_x(\xi)=T_x\cdot\xi_x
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
 q_x(\xi)=\Exp_{T_x}u_x(\xi).
\end{equation}
The change of variables $(u_x,v_x) \mapsto \xi_x=u_x+T_xv_x$ has Jacobian one.
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I=
 \frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 \int_{\R^{3n}} H(q(\xi))\,\mathcal J(\xi)
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q(\xi))|^2+|Dv(\xi)|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \,d^{3n}\xi ,
\end{equation}
where the chart factor is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J(\xi)=\prod_{x\in S} J(u_x(\xi)) 1_{|u_x(\xi)| < \pi}.
 %j(u)=
 %\begin{cases}
 %  \sin|u|/|u|,& |u|<\pi,\\
 %  0,& |u|\ge\pi,
 %\end{cases}
 %\qquad j(0)=1.
\end{equation}
%The factor $J(0)^n\mathcal J(\xi) = (4\pi)^{-n} \mathcal J(\xi)$ supplies the spherical
%Jacobian and the principal-chart restriction, while

\subsection{Gaussian coordinates}

Recall that $D: (\R^{3})^n \to (\R^3)^m$ acts on the nonfrozen coordinates.
Let $F=(F_i)_{i=1}^m\in(\R^3)^m$ be a fixed vector in the range so that
\begin{equation}
 (D\xi+F)_i=\sum_{x\in S}D_{ix}\xi_x+F_i.
\end{equation}
%
Define 
\begin{equation}
 \Delta(\xi)=|R(q(\xi))|^2+|Dv(\xi)|^2-|D\xi+F|^2.
 \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
Note that $R(q(\xi))$ is defined in terms of the physical sphere valued coordinates
whereas $D\xi+F$ is the affine ambient expression chosen for the Gaussian computation.
%
Clearly,
\begin{equation}
 e^{-(|R(q(\xi))|^2+|Dv|^2)/(2g^2)}
 =e^{-|D\xi+F|^2/(2g^2)}e^{-\Delta(\xi)/(2g^2)}.
\end{equation}
The first term on the right-hand side is a Gaussian density with
covariance $g^2A^{-1}$ where $A=D^* D$.


\subsection{Gaussian expectation identity}

It will be useful to choose a Gaussian reference measure with more general covariance $g^2C$ than $g^2 A^{-1}$.
Choose any symmetric positive definite $n\times n$ matrix $C$
and any $\mu\in(\R^3)^S$.
Let $Z\in(\R^3)^S$ have the normalized Gaussian density with
\begin{equation}
\E[Z_x^\alpha Z_y^\beta]=C_{xy}\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
\end{equation}
%
The vector $\mu\in(\R^3)^S$ is an arbitrary fixed shift in
the Gaussian coordinates
\begin{equation}
 \xi=\mu+gZ,\qquad Z\sim N(0,C\otimes I_3).
\end{equation}
The covariance $C$ has not been determined yet, but it will have finite range.

\begin{remark}
The unique minimizer of the Gaussian action
\begin{equation}
\mathcal E_F(\xi)=
\frac12|D\xi+F|^2
\end{equation}
is 
\begin{equation}
\xi = \mu_*=-A^{-1}D^*F.
\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
\begin{equation}
 \nabla_\xi\frac12|D\xi+F|^2=A\xi+D^*F,
\end{equation}
so positive definiteness of $A$ makes $\mu_*$ the unique minimizer. 
\end{proof}

This above choice $\mu=\mu_*$ gives $h=0$ in the identity below, eliminating the term linear in $Z$ in the exponent in the following proposition.
The general identity below permits other choices. The preprint uses a finite range approximation
\begin{equation}
 \mu_s=-\Pi_0^*D^*F,
\end{equation}
related to the choice $C=C_s$ where
\begin{equation}
  C_s = \frac12 (\Pi_0+\Pi_0^*),
\end{equation}
where the finite range condition is enforced by
\begin{equation}
  (\Pi_{a_0})_{yx} = 1_{y\in\Omega_x} (A_{\Omega_x} +a_0)^{-1}_{yx}.
\end{equation}
For this choice, $h=A\mu_s+D^*F$ need not vanish;
its contribution is retained explicitly.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:background-expect}
Given arbirary $F$, for any $C$ and $\mu$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal I={}&
 \kappa_g^n(\det A)^{1/2}(\det C)^{3/2}
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|r|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \\
 &\E_{\gamma_C}\left[
 H(q(\mu+gZ))\,\mathcal J(\mu+gZ)
 \exp\!\left\{
 -\frac{\Delta(\mu+gZ)}{2g^2}
 +\frac12 Z^*(C^{-1}-A)Z
 -g^{-1}(h,Z)
 \right\}\right],
 \label{eq:main}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where %$h^*Z=\sum_xh_x\cdot Z_x$ and
\begin{equation}
 r=D\mu+F,\qquad h=A\mu+D^*F,
 \qquad \kappa_g=2\pi g^2.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the above,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal I={}&
 \frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 \int_{\R^{3n}}H(q(\xi))\,\mathcal J(\xi)
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|D\xi+F|^2+\Delta(\xi)}{2g^2}\right\}d^{3n}\xi.
 \label{eq:lebesgue}
\end{align}
Now set $\xi=\mu+gZ$, whose Jacobian is $g^{3n}$.
Ordinary multiplication of squares gives
\begin{equation}
 |D(\mu+gZ)+F|^2
 =|r|^2+2g\,h^*Z+g^2Z^*AZ.
\end{equation}
The constant is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 g^{3n}(2\pi)^{3n/2}(\det C)^{3/2}
 =\kappa_g^n(\det A)^{1/2}(\det C)^{3/2}.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\section{Parametrization of the renormalized action}
\label{sec:coordinates}

The renormalized action or rather its exponential (the `retained density') is parametrized in \cite[(3.29)]{OpenAI-O3}.
The density is relative to product normalized spherical area measure $\prod_xd\omega(q_x)$.
Its representation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \rho_j(q)={}&
 e^{\mathfrak v|\Lambda_j|-b\mathcal E_{h,t}(q)}\,
 \exp\!\left\{
 -\sum_{\alpha}
       1_{X_\alpha}(q)\,\mathcal P_\alpha(q)
 -\sum_X1_X(q)f_X(q)\right\}\,\Xi(q).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
The different terms describe:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathfrak v$ is the partition function renormalization (a constant);
\item $b \mathcal E_{h,t}$ is a regularized version of the kinetic energy. If all gradients of $q$
are small then
\begin{equation}
\mathcal E_{h,t}(q) = \frac12 \langle q, K_h q\rangle
\end{equation}
and $t$ parametrizes a cutoff scale for the size of gradients.

This term is marginal and has size $g^0$. Indeed, its prefactor $b\approx 1/g^2$ and fluctuations are of size $dq^2 \approx g^2$.

\item $\mathcal P_\alpha$ are irrelevant second- to sixth-order monomials, of formal orders $g^2$ and $g^4$.
\item $f$ are the remaining irrelevant terms of size $\approx g^{4.1}$ (smaller than second order in $g^2$). 
\item $\Xi$ are nonperturbative large field terms (smaller than $e^{-(\log g)^\epsilon}$).
\end{itemize}
%
Given a field scale parameter $t>0$:
\begin{itemize}
\item 
The large gradient region (set of bad edges) is defined by
\begin{equation}
 D(q)=\{e: |d_e q|>t\}.
 \label{eq:bad-edges}
\end{equation}
\item
The indicator function that $X$ is in the small gradient region (only has good edges) is
\begin{equation}
 1_X(q)=\mathbf1\{|d_eq| \le  t\text{ for every }e\in X\}
 =\mathbf1\{X\cap D(q) = \varnothing\}.
 \label{eq:mask}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}
 The parameter is chosen as $g_j \ll t \ll g_j^{1-\epsilon}$. Typical fluctuations should be of order $g_j$.

\subsection{Renormalized kinetic energy $\mathcal E$}
\label{subsec:kinetic-energy}

The renormalized kinetic term is defined by
\begin{equation}
\exp\big\{-b\mathcal E_{h,t}(q) \big\}.
\end{equation}
Given fixed $0<d_0<c_0/16$ and $R_* \gg 1$, for any large field scale $t>0$, define
\begin{align}
 S_t(r)&=\begin{cases}
 c_0r^2/2,&r\le t,\\
 d_0tr,&r>t,
 \end{cases}\\
 m_{e,t}(q)&=\mathbf1\!\left\{
 |dq_f|\le t\,e^{\operatorname{dist}(e,f)/R_*}
                       \text{ for every edge }f\right\}.
\end{align}
Let $\mathcal E_+$ denote the positively directed nearest-neighbor edges.
The regularized kinetic energy is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_{h,t}(q)
 =
 \sum_{e\in\mathcal E_+} S_t(|d_e(q)|)+
 \frac12\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_+}m_e(q)|(\ell_hdq)_e|^2.
 \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
The constant $c_0$ and the edge operator $\ell_h$ are those from the Gaussian decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:block-gauss}:
\begin{equation}
 K_h=c_0d^*d+d^*\ell_h^*\ell_hd.
\end{equation}
If every edge is good, then $m_e=1$ for every $e$ and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_{h,t}(q)=\frac12\langle q,K_hq\rangle.
\end{equation}
Thus this is a nonlinear version of the Gaussian reference measure.
The kinetic density anchored at site $o$ is defined by (where $K=K_h$ and $\ell=\ell_h$):
\begin{equation}
 S_{K,o}(q)
   :=\frac14\sum_{\substack{e\in\mathcal E_+\\o\in\partial e}}
       \left(c_0|d_eq|^2+|(\ell dq)_e|^2\right),
 \qquad S_K=(S_{K,o})_o.
 \label{eq:kinetic-density-explicit}
\end{equation}
Summing over anchors counts each edge and row twice, so
\begin{equation}
 \sum_o S_{K,o}(q)
   =\frac12\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_+}
        \left(c_0|d_eq|^2+|(\ell dq)_e|^2\right)
   =\frac12\langle q,Kq\rangle.
 \label{eq:kinetic-density-sum}
\end{equation}


\subsection{High order irrelevant terms $f$}

The contribution is
\begin{equation}
  \exp\left\{-\sum_X 1_X(q) f_X(q) \right\}.
\end{equation}
The actitivies $f_X$ are defined for connected $X$ with support and have norms
\begin{equation}
  \|f\| = \sup_o \sum_{X \ni o} [f_X].
\end{equation}
The brackets denote the scaled local $C^k$ norm
\begin{equation}
 [f_X]_{k,j}
 =\sum_{r=0}^{k}
   \sup_{q\in\mathcal U_{X}}
   \sup_{v_1,\ldots,v_r}
   |D_{v_1,\ldots,v_r}f_X(q)|,
 \qquad 0\le k\le8.
 \label{eq:error-seminorm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal U_X$ is a set of spins containing the support of $1_X$.
Writing $v\times$ for the skew-symmetric linear map
$w\mapsto v\times w$ on $\R^3$, the rotation derivatives are
\begin{equation}
 D_{v_1,\ldots,v_r}f_X(q)
 =\left.
   \partial_{\theta_1}\cdots\partial_{\theta_r}
   f_X\!\left(\left(
     \exp\!\left[\left(\sum_{a=1}^{r}\theta_av_{a,x}\right)
                      \times\right]q_x
   \right)_x\right)
   \right|_{\theta=0}.
 \label{eq:error-rotation-jets}
\end{equation}
The supremum runs over vector fields satisfying
\begin{equation}
 |v_{a,x}|\le t(1+\operatorname{dist}(x,o))^8.
 \label{eq:error-directions}
\end{equation}
The rotations keep the spins on $S^2$.
Each derivative is scaled by $t$, so schematically this norm resembles
$\sum_{r=0}^k t^r\|\nabla^r f_X\|_\infty$, with spatial weights
and derivatives adapted to the sphere.  The exact definition is
\cite[(3.20)--(3.22)]{OpenAI-O3}.

\subsection{Nonperturbative terms $\Xi$}

The exact sum is
\begin{equation}
 \Xi(q)=\sum_{\substack{\Gamma\text{ a finite family of labels}\\
       P_\ell\cap P_{\ell'}=\varnothing\ (\ell\ne\ell')\\
       \bigsqcup_{\ell\in\Gamma}J_\ell=D(q)}}
                    \prod_{\ell\in\Gamma}k_\ell(q).
 \label{eq:covering}
\end{equation}
The polymer activities satisfy:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 
Each $k_\ell(q)$ is supported in the large field region:
\begin{equation}
 k_\ell(q)=0\quad\text{unless}\quad J_\ell\subset D(q).
\end{equation}
In particular, if $D(q)=\varnothing$ (there are no large fields), then $\Xi(q)=1$.
\item
The size of the $k_\ell$ is measured in $L^\infty$ rather than $C^8$:
\begin{equation}
 \|k\|
 = \sup_x\sum_{\ell:x\in P_\ell}
 e^{64s_\ell}\|k_\ell\|_\infty
 \label{eq:cover-bound}
\end{equation}
and eventually $\|k\| \le w=e^{-p^{1/4}}$.
\end{itemize} 

\subsection{Low order irrelevant terms $\mathcal P$}

These are the terms:
\begin{equation}
\exp\!\left\{
 -\sum_{\alpha}
       1_{X_\alpha}(q)\,\mathcal P_\alpha(q) \right\}
       .
\end{equation}
These are essentially irrelevant sixth order polynomials
written in tangent coordinates relative to a reference spin (part of $\alpha$).
They are defined in \cite[(3.13)--(3.19)]{OpenAI-O3}. 

\begin{definition}
The relative tangent projection of $q_x$ at an anchor $o$ is
\begin{equation}
 y_o(x)=q_x-(q_o\cdot q_x)q_o\in q_o^\perp.
 \label{eq:canonical-tangent}
\end{equation}
This is a projection, not the logarithmic coordinate $\Log_{q_o}q_x$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
An invariant tensor of degree $n$ is a multilinear map
$T:(\R^2)^n\to\R$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 T(Rv_1,\ldots,Rv_n)=T(v_1,\ldots,v_n)
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in O(2).
\end{equation}
The vectors $v_i$ represent tangent vectors in $q_o^\perp$ for a fixed anchor $o$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
At degree two, a basis consists of the single tensor
\begin{equation}
 T(v_1,v_2)=v_1\cdot v_2,
 \qquad T_{ab}=\delta_{ab}.
\end{equation}
At degree four, a basis consists of the three pairings
\begin{align}
 T_{12|34}(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)
   &=(v_1\cdot v_2)(v_3\cdot v_4),\\
 T_{13|24}(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)
   &=(v_1\cdot v_3)(v_2\cdot v_4),\\
 T_{14|23}(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)
   &=(v_1\cdot v_4)(v_2\cdot v_3).
\end{align}
For instance, $(T_{12|34})_{abcd}=\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}$.
%These are the tensor types in the quartic polynomial below.
At degree six, examples are
\begin{equation}
 (v_1\cdot v_2)(v_3\cdot v_4)(v_5\cdot v_6),
 \qquad
 (v_1\cdot v_3)(v_2\cdot v_5)(v_4\cdot v_6).
\end{equation}
All fifteen pairings span the degree-six invariant space.  In
dimension two they have linear relations, so a basis is obtained
by choosing a linearly independent subset.  Odd degrees have no
nonzero invariant tensors: applying $R=-I$ gives
\begin{equation}
 T(v_1,\ldots,v_n)=(-1)^nT(v_1,\ldots,v_n).
\end{equation}
The basis of tensor types is fixed once and for all.
\end{example}

Let $q_o\in S^2$ and let $T:(\R^2)^n\to\R$ be an
$O(2)$-invariant multilinear tensor.  For $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in q_o^\perp$
and any linear isometry $\iota:q_o^\perp\to\R^2$, the value
\begin{equation}
 T_{q_o}(v_1,\ldots,v_n):=T(\iota v_1,\ldots,\iota v_n)
\end{equation}
is independent of $\iota$.  Thus $T$ defines a multilinear tensor
on the tangent plane without choosing an orthonormal frame.

The corresponding global covariance rotates the anchor spin and
all tangent vectors simultaneously:
\begin{equation}
 T_{Rq_o}(Rv_1,\ldots,Rv_n)=T_{q_o}(v_1,\ldots,v_n),
 \qquad R\in O(3).
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $\iota$ is an orthonormal identification at $q_o$, then
$\iota R^{-1}$ is one at $Rq_o$.  For tangent vectors obtained
from physical spins, the same covariance follows from
\begin{equation}
 \proj_{(Rq_o)^\perp}(Rq_x)
 =R\proj_{q_o^\perp}q_x.
\end{equation}
Thus a simultaneous global transformation of the spin configuration
leaves each invariant polynomial unchanged.  Holding the anchor
spin fixed restricts this symmetry to its stabilizer,
\begin{equation}
 \{R\in O(3):Rq_o=q_o\}\cong O(2),
\end{equation}
which is the $O(2)$ acting on tangent vectors in the definition of $T_\alpha$. 

\begin{definition}
A monomial label 
\begin{equation}
\alpha = (o_\alpha,n_\alpha,\varepsilon_\alpha,a_\alpha,{\bf r}_\alpha,T_\alpha, X_\alpha, S_\alpha, R_\alpha,s_\alpha)
\end{equation}
specifies an anchor $o_\alpha$, a degree $n_\alpha$, a coefficient
$a_\alpha$, displacements $r_1^\alpha,\ldots,r_{n_\alpha}^\alpha\in\mathbb Z^2$, and an
$O(2)$-invariant multilinear tensor $T_\alpha$.  Writing $o=o_\alpha$,
its monomial is
\begin{equation}
 a_\alpha T_\alpha\bigl(y_o(o+r_1^\alpha),\ldots,y_o(o+r_{n_\alpha}^\alpha)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The tensors $T_\alpha$ are chosen from a fixed basis of the invariant tensor space.
The label also contains the following geometric data:
\begin{itemize}
\item the mask graph $X_\alpha$ is a connected graph
containing the anchor $o$ and the sites $o+r_i^\alpha$, with the
joining paths, enlargements, and complete record $R_\alpha$ prescribed by its construction;
%
\item the complete support $S_\alpha$ is a set of vertices containing every spin
argument and every site or edge contained in $X_\alpha$ and extended by the range of the finite-range covariances;
\item
the record $R_\alpha$ is a connected collection of cubes of radius
$\mathfrak m_j=\lceil(\log H_j)^2\rceil$ and joining paths covering
$S_\alpha$ and containing $X_\alpha$.  It retains path multiplicities
and, on a torus, displacements before reduction modulo the periods
\item its integer load $s_\alpha\ge1$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 |S_\alpha|\le C\mathfrak m_j^2s_\alpha,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{length}(R_\alpha)\le C\mathfrak m_js_\alpha,
\end{equation}
where $|S_\alpha|$ counts sites, including endpoints of queried edges.
The load need not be minimal.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

% Zero displacements give zero monomials and odd degrees vanish by $O(2)$ invariance.

Each term $\mathcal P_\alpha$ in \eqref{eq:density} is of the form
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal P_\alpha(q)=b^{\varepsilon_\alpha}a_\alpha
 \begin{cases}
 T_\alpha\bigl(y_o(o+r_1),\ldots,y_o(o+r_n)\bigr),&n=4,6,\\
 Q_{r,s}(q),&n=2,
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:canonical-label}
\end{equation}
They are grouped into the following slots:
\begin{equation}
 (bP_4,\ I_2,\ bP_5,\ I_3,\ bP_6,\ I_4,\ b^{-1}J_2),
 \qquad P_5=I_3=0,
 \label{eq:canonical-slots}
\end{equation}
%where $P_4,I_2,P_6,I_4,J_2$ denote coefficient lists, with indicating their degree.
%The slot determines $(n_\alpha,\varepsilon_\alpha)$:
where
\begin{equation}
 \begin{array}{c|ccccc}
 \text{slot}&bP_4&I_2&bP_6&I_4&b^{-1}J_2\\ \hline
 n&4&2&6&4&2\\
 \varepsilon_\alpha&1&0&1&0&-1\\
 \text{formal order for }y=O(g),\ b=g^{-2}
   &g^2&g^2&g^4&g^4&g^4
 \end{array}
\end{equation}
%
Let $\mathcal X_j$ denote all possible graphs and $\mathcal A_j(X)=\{\alpha:X_\alpha=X\}$ be the labels with graph $X$.  Then 
\begin{equation}
 \sum_\alpha1_{X_\alpha}(q)\mathcal P_\alpha(q)
 =\sum_{X\in\mathcal X_j}1_X(q)
      \sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal A_j(X)}\mathcal P_\alpha(q).
 \label{eq:canonical-graph-sum}
\end{equation}
% The quadratic labels in $I_2,J_2$ are compensated packets.  Let
% $\mathcal C_4$ be the four quarter-turn rotations of the square lattice
% and define
% \begin{align}
%  S_o(q)&=\frac14\sum_{|e|_1=1}|y_o(o+e)|^2,\\
%  Q_{r,s}(q)&=\frac14\sum_{\rho\in\mathcal C_4}
%  y_o(o+\rho r)\cdot y_o(o+\rho s)-(r\cdot s)S_o(q).
%  \label{eq:canonical-quadratic-packet}
% \end{align}
% For an affine tangent field $y_o(o+r)=Ar$, the two terms in
% $Q_{r,s}$ agree, so $Q_{r,s}=0$.  This removes the leading kinetic
% component, whose coefficient is tracked by $b$.  The entire packet,
% including its nearest-neighbor compensation, retains a common mask
% and complete record.
%
Restricting to degree $n$, the polynomial interaction is
\begin{equation}
 A_n(q)=\sum_\alpha 1_{X_\alpha}(q)\,a_\alpha
 T_\alpha\bigl(y_{o(\alpha)}(o(\alpha)+r_1^\alpha),\ldots,
               y_{o(\alpha)}(o(\alpha)+r_n^\alpha)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The kernels have exponential decay.
With $W_\sigma(u)=e^{\sigma u}(1+u)^2$, the degree-four and -six norms are
\begin{equation}
 \|A_n\|_\sigma
 =\sup_o\sum_{\alpha\text{ at }o}
 |a_\alpha|W_\sigma\!\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^n|r_i|_1\right).
 \label{eq:canonical-coefficient-norm}
\end{equation}
Here $A_n$ denotes the entire degree-$n$ coefficient list
$(a_\alpha)_\alpha$, together with its labels.  Each label specifies
an anchor $o_\alpha$, displacements $r_i^\alpha$, an invariant
tensor $T_\alpha$, and support data. 

Thus the norm sums the individual coefficients of $A_n$ with
spatial weights.  For example, $A_4$ can stand for $P_4$ or $I_4$,
and $A_6$ for $P_6$; the displayed powers of $b$ in the action are
excluded.  
For a quadratic list, the corresponding contribution of $aQ_{r,s}$ is
\begin{equation}
 |a|\bigl\{W_\sigma(1+|r|_1+|s|_1)
                 +|r\cdot s|W_\sigma(3)\bigr\},
\end{equation}
summed over labels and with the same supremum over anchors.

\subsubsection{Example}

For example, the quartic polynomial anchored at $o$ can be written
explicitly using the three $O(2)$-invariant dot-product pairings.
Writing $\mathbf r=(r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4)$ and $y_i=y_o(o+r_i)$,
and displaying the polynomial without its graph masks, we have
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 P_{4,o}(y)=\sum_{\mathbf r\in(\mathbb Z^2)^4}\bigl[&
 a^{12|34}_{o;\mathbf r}(y_1\cdot y_2)(y_3\cdot y_4)\\
 &+a^{13|24}_{o;\mathbf r}(y_1\cdot y_3)(y_2\cdot y_4)\\
 &+a^{14|23}_{o;\mathbf r}(y_1\cdot y_4)(y_2\cdot y_3)\bigr].
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:quartic-anchor-polynomial}
\end{equation}
The $a^{ij|kl}_{o;\mathbf r}$ are the quartic coefficients, with
the action prefactor $b$ excluded.  In the retained representation,
each coefficient keeps its graph label and mask; these extra labels
are suppressed here.  Summing the masked terms over anchors gives
$P_4$, whose contribution to the action is $bP_4$.

These coefficients are generally not finite range.  The displacement
tuple ranges over $(\mathbb Z^2)^4$; terms with any $r_i=0$ vanish
because $y_o(o)=0$.  Instead of finite range, the coefficient norm
requires exponential spatial decay:
\begin{equation}
 \sup_o\sum_{\alpha:\,o(\alpha)=o}
 |a_\alpha|\,
 e^{\sigma(1+\sum_{i=1}^4|r_i^\alpha|_1)}
 \left(2+\sum_{i=1}^4|r_i^\alpha|_1\right)^2\le C.
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha$ includes the pairing type and all additional retained
labels of the quartic coefficient.  Interactions reaching far from
the anchor are therefore small but need not vanish.  The finite
range of the fluctuation covariance $C_s$ does not imply finite
range of these coefficients: their canonical perturbative calculation
uses full kernels.  On a finite torus the positions are folded
periodically, while the decay bounds use the lifted displacement
records.

The same polynomial can be expressed directly in the spin variables.
Since $|q_o|=1$, define
\begin{equation}
 G_o(x,z):=y_o(x)\cdot y_o(z)
 =q_x\cdot q_z-(q_o\cdot q_x)(q_o\cdot q_z).
\end{equation}
Writing $x_i=o+r_i$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 P_{4,o}(q)=\sum_{\mathbf r\in(\mathbb Z^2)^4}\bigl[&
 a^{12|34}_{o;\mathbf r}G_o(x_1,x_2)G_o(x_3,x_4)\\
 &+a^{13|24}_{o;\mathbf r}G_o(x_1,x_3)G_o(x_2,x_4)\\
 &+a^{14|23}_{o;\mathbf r}G_o(x_1,x_4)G_o(x_2,x_3)\bigr].
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:quartic-anchor-spins}
\end{equation}
The same graph masks are attached to the retained labels.
Global $O(3)$ invariance is explicit because only scalar products
of spins occur.  The word ``quartic'' refers to degree in the
relative tangent coordinates, not degree in all the ambient spin
variables $q$.

\section{Extraction and localization}
\label{sec:extraction-localization}

This section defines the extraction operations on effective action densities.
%
The presentation is inspired by the localization by matching polynomial
test functions in \cite{MR3332939}.

Section~\ref{sec:perturbative-coupling} applies these operations
in the computation of perturbation theory.  Throughout this section we work at
a single scale, with a fixed normalized kinetic term $K$ and its kinetic density $S_K$,
see \eqref{eq:kinetic-density-sum}.

\subsection{Effective action densities}
\label{subsec:action-densities}

Define the term \emph{action density} to mean a collection $G=(G_o)_o$ 
of functions of $q$ assigned to one lattice anchor $o$.
The corresponding action contribution is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal G_\Lambda(q)
   =\sum_{o\in\Lambda}   G_o(q).
\end{equation}
%
For each anchor $o$ and $q_o\in S^2$,
the arguments of $G_o$ are expanded in relative tangent coordinates
$y_o(x)\in q_o^\perp$, with $y_o(o)=0$, defined in
\eqref{eq:canonical-tangent}.
%
% One possible precise domain for an actual, rather than formal, density is
% \begin{equation}
%  \mathcal Y_o(q_o)
%    =\{y_o\in\ell^\infty(\mathbb Z^2;q_o^\perp):y_o(o)=0\},
%  \qquad
%  G_o(q_o;\,\cdot):
%  B_\varepsilon(0;\mathcal Y_o(q_o))\longrightarrow\R,
%  \quad 0<\varepsilon<1,
%  \label{eq:loc-density-domain}
% \end{equation}
% where $G_o$ is twice continuously differentiable near zero.
% Only its germ at zero is used. 
In this chart the associated spin configuration is
\begin{equation}
 q_x=\sqrt{1-|y_o(x)|^2}\,q_o+y_o(x),
 \qquad
 y_o(x)=\proj_{q_o^\perp}q_x.
 \label{eq:loc-density-spin-chart}
\end{equation}
The corresponding action contribution is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal G_\Lambda(q)
   =\sum_{o\in\Lambda}
       G_o\bigl(q_o;(\proj_{q_o^\perp}q_x)_{x\in\mathbb Z^2}\bigr),
 \label{eq:loc-density-sum}
\end{equation}
whenever the fields lie in the relevant charts.


\begin{definition}
For a bulk density $G=(G_o)_o$, define its homogeneous degree-$n$
Taylor part by varying the tangent field while holding the
anchor spin $q_o$ fixed:
\begin{equation}
 (\Tay_n G_o)(q_o;y)
   =\frac1{n!}\left.\frac{d^n}{dt^n}G_o(q_o;ty)\right|_{t=0},
 \qquad n\geq0.
 \label{eq:kinetic-loc-taylor}
\end{equation}
The fixed parameter $q_o$ is usually supposed in subsequent formulas.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Localization operator}
\label{subsec:localization}

%A density $G$ can contain several Taylor degrees, and even its
%quadratic part need not be a multiple of the kinetic term.
The localization operator extracts the kinetic coefficient of a
density's quadratic Taylor part by matching it on affine tangent
test functions.

\begin{definition}[Affine tangent test functions]
\label{def:affine-test-functions}
Fix an anchor site $o\in\Z^2$ and an anchor spin $q_o\in S^2$.
An affine tangent test function anchored at $o$ is a field
$y_o:\Z^2\to q_o^\perp$ of the form
\begin{equation}
 y_o(o+r)=\mathsf L_o r,\qquad r\in\Z^2,\qquad
 \mathsf L_o:\R^2\longrightarrow q_o^\perp,
 \label{eq:affine-test-functions}
\end{equation}
where $\mathsf L_o$ is any real-linear map.  Its input $r$ is a
spatial displacement and its output is a tangent-spin displacement.
The map is defined on $\R^2$ and evaluated at integer
displacements.
\end{definition}

To make this test function concrete, choose two tangent vectors
$a_1,a_2\in q_o^\perp$ and write
\begin{equation}
 \mathsf L_o(r_1,r_2)=r_1a_1+r_2a_2,
 \qquad
 \frac12\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o)
 =\frac12\bigl(|a_1|^2+|a_2|^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
The vectors $a_1,a_2$ are the constant increments of the affine
tangent field in the two spatial directions.  This is the meaning
of its slope; $\mathsf L_o$ is not a projection.


\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:kinetic-normalization}
Let $S_{K}$ be the kinetic density of
Section~\ref{subsec:kinetic-energy}, with normalization $K(p)=|p|^2+O(|p|^4)$.
Then the quadratic part of $S_{K,o}$ has unit affine normalization:
\begin{equation}
 (\Tay_2 S_{K,o})(\mathsf L_o r)
   =\tfrac12\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o),
 \label{eq:kinetic-loc-normalization}
\end{equation}
where $\mathsf L_o r = (\mathsf L_o r)_{r \in \Z^2}$ and
the right-hand side is independent of $r$ (since $\mathsf L_o r$
has constant gradients).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Use the factorization $K=c_0d^*d+d^*\ell^*\ell d$ from
Section~\ref{sec:block-gauss}.  Write $\widehat\ell(0)$ for the
real $2\times2$ Fourier symbol at zero, acting on the two edge
directions.  Since $d(p)=ip+O(|p|^2)$, the quadratic momentum
term of $K(p)$ gives
\begin{equation}
 c_0 I+\widehat\ell(0)^*\widehat\ell(0)=I.
\end{equation}
Set $a_\mu=\mathsf L_o e_\mu$.  For the affine tangent field
$y_o(o+r)=\mathsf L_o r$, the discrete gradient along the edge
from $o+r$ to $o+r+e_\mu$ is
\begin{equation}
 (dy_o)_{(o+r,o+r+e_\mu)}
 =y_o(o+r+e_\mu)-y_o(o+r)
 =\mathsf L_o(r+e_\mu)-\mathsf L_o r
 =a_\mu.
\end{equation}
The displacement $r$ cancels, so the gradient depends only on
the direction $\mu$, not on the edge's location.
Thus $dy_o$ is a constant edge field with directional values
$a=(a_1,a_2)$.  Translation invariance of $\ell$ then implies
that $\ell dy_o$ is also constant, with directional values
$(\widehat\ell(0)a)_\mu$: the symbol at zero is the action of
$\ell$ on constant edge fields.
%
By \eqref{eq:kinetic-density-explicit} therefore
\begin{equation}
 (\Tay_2 S_{K,o})(\mathsf L_o r)
 =\frac12\sum_{\mu=1}^2
       \left(c_0|a_\mu|^2+|(\widehat\ell(0)a)_\mu|^2\right)
 =\frac12\sum_{\mu=1}^2|a_\mu|^2.
\end{equation}
This is the asserted trace.  
\end{proof}

% The normalization concerns variation around a constant spin
% configuration.  At the constant configuration the kinetic
% density itself vanishes; its quadratic variation under affine
% tangent perturbations determines the kinetic coefficient.

\begin{definition}[Localization operator]
\label{def:kinetic-loc}
Use the kinetic density $S_{K,o}$ of
Section~\ref{subsec:kinetic-energy}.
%On densities $G$ for which the following affine evaluations
%are defined and can be matched,
Define $\Loc_K G$ separately at
each anchor: $(\Loc_K G)_o$ is the unique element of
$\operatorname{span}\{S_{K,o}\}$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \Bigl[\Tay_2\bigl(G_o-(\Loc_{K}G)_o\bigr)\Bigr]
                      (\mathsf L_o r)=0
 \quad\text{for every }\mathsf L_o:\R^2\to q_o^\perp.
 \label{eq:kinetic-loc-matching}
\end{equation}
The operator depends only on the quadratic Taylor part of $G$, but
returns, at each anchor, a multiple of the full nonlinear
kinetic density $S_{K,o}$.
%The multiplier may depend on $o$;
%translation covariance is not required for this definition.
%Evaluation on an affine test function is algebraic; the unbounded
%affine field need not lie in a small-field chart.
Uniqueness follows from \eqref{eq:kinetic-loc-normalization}.
\end{definition}

For $G$ in the domain of $\Loc_K$, denote its kinetic coefficient
at anchor $o$ by $\mathfrak a_o(G)$:
\begin{equation}
 (\Loc_K G)_o=\mathfrak a_o(G)S_{K,o}.
 \label{eq:kinetic-loc}
\end{equation}
The anchor spin $q_o$ is held fixed in this definition;
dependence of $\mathfrak a_o(G)$ on that parameter is suppressed.


\begin{proposition}[Symmetries of the kinetic coefficient]
\label{prop:kinetic-coefficient-symmetries}
Suppose $G$ is in the domain of $\Loc_K$ at every anchor and
anchor spin, and write $\mathfrak a_o(G;n)$ when the anchor spin
is $n\in S^2$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If each density is globally $O(3)$-invariant, meaning
\begin{equation}
 G_o(Rn;Ry)=G_o(n;y)
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in O(3),
\end{equation}
then $\mathfrak a_o(G;n)$ is independent of $n$.
\item If the family is translation covariant, meaning
\begin{equation}
 G_{o+z}(n;\tau_z y)=G_o(n;y),
 \qquad
 (\tau_z y)_{o+z}(o+z+r)=y_o(o+r),
 \qquad z,r\in\Z^2,
\end{equation}
then $\mathfrak a_o(G;n)$ is independent of $o$ for each fixed $n$.
\end{enumerate}
If both properties hold, the coefficient is independent of
both the anchor site and the anchor spin, and is denoted
$\mathfrak a(G)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For any nonzero slope $\mathsf L:\R^2\to n^\perp$, affine
matching and unit normalization give
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak a_o(G;n)
 =\frac{2(\Tay_2G_o)(n;(\mathsf L r)_{r\in\Z^2})}
        {\tr(\mathsf L^*\mathsf L)}.
\end{equation}
A rotation replaces $(n,\mathsf L)$ by $(Rn,R\mathsf L)$.
Global invariance preserves the numerator, and orthogonality
preserves the denominator.  Since $O(3)$ acts transitively on
$S^2$, the coefficient is independent of $n$.
A translation replaces $o$ by $o+z$ while keeping the
relative displacement $r$, the spin $n$, and the slope
$\mathsf L$ fixed.  Translation covariance therefore preserves
the same quotient, proving independence of $o$.
\end{proof}

The following proposition gives sufficient conditions for existence.


\begin{proposition}[Existence of kinetic localization]
\label{prop:kinetic-symmetry}
Let $G=(G_o)_o$ be a translation-covariant family of bulk
densities on $\mathbb Z^2$, with each density globally
$O(3)$-invariant, in the sense of
Proposition~\ref{prop:kinetic-coefficient-symmetries}.
Suppose in addition:
\begin{enumerate}
\item each quadratic density $\Tay_2G_o$ is invariant under
lattice rotations about the anchor $o$;
\item the quadratic coefficients have a finite absolute second
displacement moment:
\begin{equation}
 (\Tay_2 G_o)(y)=\sum_{r,s\in\mathbb Z^2}a_{r,s}\,
              y_o(o+r)\cdot y_o(o+s),
 \qquad
 \sum_{r,s}|a_{r,s}|(1+|r|_1+|s|_1)^2<\infty.
 \label{eq:affine-hessian-domain}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Then the affine evaluations converge and $\Loc_K G$ exists.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:kinetic-coefficient-symmetries}, its
coefficient $\alpha_G=\mathfrak a(G)$ is independent of the
anchor site and spin, and
\begin{equation}
 (\Tay_2 G_o)(\mathsf L_o r)
 =\frac{\alpha_G}2\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o)
 =\frac{\alpha_G}2\bigl(|a_1|^2+|a_2|^2\bigr).
 \label{eq:kinetic-symmetry}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It remains to prove existence at each fixed anchor and spin.
Evaluate $\Tay_2 G_o$ on $y_o(o+r)=r_1a_1+r_2a_2$.
The absolute second moment makes this evaluation summable.
The stabilizer of the anchor spin gives internal $O(2)$
invariance, so the resulting quadratic form
is a linear combination of dot products:
\begin{equation}
 Q(a_1,a_2)
 =c_1|a_1|^2+2c_{12}a_1\cdot a_2+c_2|a_2|^2.
\end{equation}
A spatial quarter turn exchanges the slopes up to a sign, so
\begin{equation}
 Q(a_1,a_2)=Q(a_2,-a_1)
 =c_1|a_2|^2-2c_{12}a_1\cdot a_2+c_2|a_1|^2.
\end{equation}
Since this holds for arbitrary $a_1,a_2$, it forces
$c_1=c_2$ and $c_{12}=0$.  Thus $2c_1S_{K,o}$ satisfies
the matching condition \eqref{eq:kinetic-loc-matching}, proving
existence.  Proposition~\ref{prop:kinetic-coefficient-symmetries}
then identifies these local coefficients with a common
$\alpha_G=\mathfrak a(G)$ and gives \eqref{eq:kinetic-symmetry}.
\end{proof}

Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:kinetic-symmetry},
the explicit coefficient formula follows from affine matching:
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak a(G)=\mathfrak a(\Tay_2 G)
              =\sum_{r,s}a_{r,s}(r\cdot s).
 \label{eq:affine-hessian-moment}
\end{equation}
\begin{proof}
Choose an isometry $\mathsf L_o:\R^2\to q_o^\perp$.
Then $\tfrac12\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o)=1$ and
$(\mathsf L_o r)\cdot(\mathsf L_o s)=r\cdot s$.
Substitution of \eqref{eq:affine-hessian-domain} and
\eqref{eq:kinetic-loc} into the matching condition
\eqref{eq:kinetic-loc-matching}, using the unit normalization
\eqref{eq:kinetic-loc-normalization}, gives
\eqref{eq:affine-hessian-moment}; the absolute second moment
justifies the sum.
\end{proof}

The operator is a linear projection, and its remainder has zero
affine coefficient at every anchor:
\begin{equation}
 \Loc_{K}^2=\Loc_{K},\qquad
 \mathfrak a_o\bigl((\Id-\Loc_K)G\bigr)=0
 \quad\text{for every }o.
 \label{eq:kinetic-loc-projection}
\end{equation}
Both identities follow immediately from
$\mathfrak a_o(S_K)=\mathfrak a_o(\Tay_2 S_K)=1$ at each anchor.

For families satisfying both symmetry assumptions of
Proposition~\ref{prop:kinetic-coefficient-symmetries}, we use
the common coefficient $\mathfrak a(G)$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathfrak a_o(G)=\mathfrak a(G)\quad\text{for every }o,
 \qquad
 \Loc_K G=\mathfrak a(G)S_K.
 \label{eq:kinetic-loc-translation}
\end{equation}
For such families, the range is $\operatorname{span}\{S_K\}$.

\section{Perturbation theory and the running coupling}
\label{sec:perturbative-coupling}

We combine the fluctuation integral of
Section~\ref{sec:background-field} with the extraction and
localization operations of Section~\ref{sec:extraction-localization}
to compute one renormalization group step in the sense of formal perturbation theory.

Block lattice anchors are denoted by $O\in\Lambda'$; the notation $o$ in the preceding
sections denotes a site anchor on the fine lattice.
%The calculation follows the canonical coefficient prescription in
%\cite[Section~5.5]{OpenAI-O3}: take connected Gaussian expectations, extract
%the kinetic term, and reassemble the remaining Taylor coefficients.
The incoming regular errors $f_X$ are set to zero and $\Xi=1$,
and the bulk coefficients are computed around a slowly varying spin configuration.
%The output field is allowed to vary near that configuration;
%its quadratic variation determines the kinetic increment.

A step goes from $j$ to $j-1$.  With $h=N-j$ completed blocking
steps, use
\begin{equation}
 b=b_j,\qquad g=b^{-1/2},\qquad K=K_h,\qquad K'=K_{h+1}.
\end{equation}
The incoming action on the small-gradient region is (see Section~\ref{sec:coordinates})
\begin{equation}
 b\mathcal E(q)+\mathcal W(q),\qquad
 \mathcal E(q)=\tfrac12\langle q,Kq\rangle,\qquad
 \mathcal W=bP_4+I_2+bP_6+I_4+b^{-1}J_2.
\end{equation}
The Gaussian integration first produces a raw output interaction
$\mathcal W''$ relative to $b\mathcal E'(V)$, where
$\mathcal E'(V)=\tfrac12\langle V,K'V\rangle$.
We then apply the single-scale operator $\Loc_{K'}$ from
Section~\ref{subsec:localization} with kinetic term $K'$.
%Whenever localization or $\mathfrak a$ is applied to an
%interaction, it acts on its anchored density family before
%summation over anchors; we use the same symbol for the family
%and its summed action.

The formal calculation drops terms of order higher than four after
rescaling tangent fields by $g$.  Modulo field-independent
terms, its result has the form
\begin{equation}
 b\mathcal E'(V)+\mathcal W''(V)
 =b^-\mathcal E'(V)+\mathcal W'(V)+\mathcal R'(V),
 \qquad b^-=b+\alpha+\frac{\kappa}{b},
 \label{eq:pert-formal-reassembly}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal W'$ is in the retained canonical slots and
$\mathcal R'$ contains the omitted formal orders.

Each term in $\mathcal W$ (incoming slot) is a sum of labelled terms with
anchors $o_\alpha$, as in \eqref{eq:canonical-label}.
Its tangent arguments are the relative projections
$y_{o_\alpha}(x)$ of \eqref{eq:canonical-tangent}, whose
reference spin is $q_{o_\alpha}$. 
%
The same holds for the renormalized $\mathcal W'$ with block anchors $O_\alpha$.

During the fluctuation integration, the anchored spin
$q_{o_\alpha}=\Exp_{T_{o_\alpha}}u_{o_\alpha}$ varies with the
integration variables, whereas the reference spin $T_{o_\alpha}=V_{[o_\alpha]}$
is fixed by the block-spin field.  Thus all terms in $\mathcal W$
are evaluated on the same spin configuration $q$,
but each uses its own anchor spin and relative tangent coordinates.

\subsection{Fluctuation integral}

Throughout we assume that all gradients are small and that there no bad regions.
Start from the coordinates and Gaussian identity of Section~\ref{sec:background-field}:
\begin{equation}
 A=D^*D=K+aQ^*Q,\qquad C=A^{-1},
\end{equation}
where we use $a=1$,
no finite-range covariance truncation is made,
and
\begin{equation}
 \mu=-CD^*F,\qquad \xi=\mu+gZ,\qquad
 Z\sim N(0,C\otimes I_3).
\end{equation}
Given the Section~\ref{sec:background-field} reference spins
$T_i=V_{O(i)}$, the mean is chosen in terms of
\begin{equation}
 F_i=\proj_{T_i^\perp}R_i(0,V).
\end{equation}
The notation means that $R_i(0,V)\in \R^3$ is evaluated with zero intrinsic tangent
coordinates, i.e., their fine spins equal their block references $T_x=V_{[x]}$. 
{\red [Explain this choice.]}

Equation~\eqref{eq:cEqV} extracts $b\mathcal E'(V)$ with the incoming coupling $b$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:background-expect}
then expresses the remaining interaction, modulo constants, as
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W''(V)
   =-\log\E_C\left[e^{-\mathcal V(V,Z)}
                         e^{-\mathcal W(q(V,Z))}\right],
 \label{eq:pert-connected-action}
\end{equation}
where $q(V,Z)$ is the spin configuration reconstructed from $\xi=\mu(V)+gZ$ and the
geometric part of the fluctuation integral is given by Proposition~\ref{prop:background-expect} by
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal V(V,Z)
 =\frac b2\sum_i
   \bigl(|R_i(q)|^2+|D_iv|^2-|D_i\xi+F_i|^2\bigr)
   +b\mathcal L(q,V)+\frac b2|D\mu+F|^2
 -\sum_x\log\frac{J(u_x)}{J(0)}.
   \label{eq:pert-vertices}
\end{equation}
%
The Jacobian is
\begin{equation}
 -\log\frac{J(u)}{J(0)}
 =-\log\frac{\sin|u|}{|u|}
 =\frac{|u|^2}{6}+\frac{|u|^4}{180}+O(|u|^6).
\end{equation}
The term $\mathcal L$ is the defect between the linear and normalized block average in \eqref{eq:cEqV}.






\begin{proposition}[Symmetry]
\label{prop:spin-symmetry}
For a globally $O(3)$-invariant input interaction $\mathcal W$,
the output density and the raw effective interaction $\mathcal W''$
are invariant under global
$O(3)$ transformations of the spins.
The connected Taylor coefficients inherit this invariance. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $R\in O(3)$, the block mean obeys
$\widehat m(Rq)=R\widehat m(q)$.  %, with the same tag if needed.
The normalized block-spin kernel consequently satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal B(RV,Rq)=\mathcal B(V,q).
\end{equation}
Invariance of the incoming density and spherical measure gives,
by $q=R\widetilde q$,
\begin{equation}
 \rho'(RV)
 =\int\rho(q)\mathcal B(RV,q)\,d\omega(q)
 =\int\rho(\widetilde q)\mathcal B(V,\widetilde q)
                      \,d\omega(\widetilde q)
 =\rho'(V).
\end{equation}
Here the integrals use product spherical measure and include the
tag average when needed.

The perturbative representation preserves the same symmetry.
Indeed, the intrinsic coordinates and tangent projections satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \Exp_{RT}(Ru)=R\Exp_T(u),\qquad
 \proj_{(RT)^\perp}(R\xi)=R\proj_{T^\perp}\xi.
\end{equation}
The projected affine rows are equivariant and the Gaussian covariance
is $C\otimes I_3$.  Thus $RZ$ has the same law as $Z$, and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal V(RV,RZ)=\mathcal V(V,Z),\qquad
 \mathcal W''(RV)=\mathcal W''(V).
\end{equation}
The incoming factor has the same invariance, since
$q(RV,RZ)=Rq(V,Z)$ and $\mathcal W(Rq)=\mathcal W(q)$.
Taking the connected expansion and each homogeneous Taylor part
preserves these identities.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Connected Wick contractions}
\label{subsec:pert-connected}

Write $\langle X_1;\ldots;X_m\rangle_c$ for a cumulant with
respect to the Gaussian field $Z$, holding the block spin field $V$ fixed.
The cumulants act on the total interaction
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z)
   :=\mathcal V(V,Z)+\mathcal W(q(V,Z)).
 \label{eq:pert-total-interaction}
\end{equation}
This includes mixed contractions between the geometric vertices
and the incoming polynomial terms.  The expansion is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal W''(V)
 ={}&\langle\mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z)\rangle
  -\frac12\langle\mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z);
                    \mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z)\rangle_c\\
 &+\frac16\langle\mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z);
                 \mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z);
                 \mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z)\rangle_c\\
 &-\frac1{24}\langle\mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z);
                    \mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z);
                    \mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z);
                    \mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}(V,Z)\rangle_c+\cdots.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:pert-cumulants}
\end{equation}
%
After expanding the vertices in $\mathcal V_{\mathrm{tot}}$ into monomials and applying Wick's
rule, each output term corresponds to a Feynman diagram: its
vertices are the input monomials, its internal lines are Gaussian
covariance contractions, and its external legs are the remaining
field factors.  A connected output term means that this diagram
is connected, so that its input vertices are joined by paths of
internal lines.  The cumulants retain only connected diagrams.



\subsection{Block spin anchoring}


The incoming terms have anchors $o\in\Lambda$.
The output will be organized analogously with block anchors $O\in\Lambda'$.
Each contribution is distributed equally among its permitted coarse anchors \cite[Section~3]{OpenAI-O3}
as follows: For a diagram $G_\Gamma(V)$,
let $A_\Gamma$ be its nonempty finite set of permitted anchors.
Set
\begin{equation}
 G_{\Gamma,O}(V)
   =\frac{\mathbf1_{\{O\in A_\Gamma\}}}{|A_\Gamma|}G_\Gamma(V),
 \qquad
 \sum_O G_{\Gamma,O}(V)=G_\Gamma(V).
\end{equation}
% Each assigned copy retains the same complete support. 
The permitted-anchor sets transform with the diagram, so equal
distribution commutes with translations and lattice rotations.
Write $\mathcal W''_O$ for the sum of the contributions assigned
to $O$.  We introduce this assignment before the coefficient
calculation below, but it is applied to connected outputs,
including contractions between vertices with different input anchors.

\paragraph{Reference block spin configuration}

The anchored contribution $\mathcal W''_O$ is expressed 
the relative tangent coordinates of \eqref{eq:canonical-tangent}, with $q_x,q_o$ replaced by $V_Y,V_O$.
The summed interaction is unchanged by the anchoring, but its
relative coordinates and anchored Taylor coefficients depend on $O$.  

For the Taylor expansion at $O$, take $n=V_O$ as the reference
spin.  Since $y_O(O)=0$ identically, the rescaled relative
coordinates have $\eta_O=0$.  Consider the family
\begin{equation}
 V_Y(\varepsilon)
   =\sqrt{1-\varepsilon^2|\eta_Y|^2}\,n+\varepsilon\eta_Y,
 \qquad \eta_Y\in n^\perp,\qquad \eta_O=0.
 \label{eq:pert-external-field-family}
\end{equation}

anchored density.  It fixes the anchor spin while allowing the

rotation $R$ with $RV_O=n$ gives
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}
Thus global $O(3)$ invariance allows the anchor spin to be fixed
without losing information about the invariant density.

The block-spin field $V(\varepsilon)$ is held fixed during the Gaussian integration.
Expanding the resulting output density at $\varepsilon=0$
measures its response to spatially varying perturbations of the

 \label{eq:pert-vertex-orders}
\end{align}
The incoming interaction starts at order $g^2$ by the slot

 \label{eq:pert-sorted}
\end{equation}

\end{equation}

The terms $F_{r,d}$ have field degree $r+2d$, so $g^{-2d}F_{r,d}$
has formal order $g^r$.  Thus the field degrees are $4,2,6,4,2$.

\begin{equation}
 g^{-2d}F_{r,d}(g\eta)=g^rF_{r,d}(\eta).
\end{equation}
Thus the $F_{r,d}$ are obtained by two distinct extractions:
$[g^r]$ selects formal perturbative order, and $\Tay_{r+2d}$
selects degree in the external variable $\eta$:
\begin{equation}
 F_{r,d}(\eta)
   =\Tay_{r+2d}\bigl([g^r]\mathcal W''(V(g\eta))\bigr),

 \label{eq:pert-taylor-slots}
\end{equation}

The first index $r$ records the perturbative order, while the second index $d$

$4,2$ at order two and $6,4,2$ at order four.

\paragraph{From the formal recursion to the exact one}

Write $\mathbf P$ for the collection of retained polynomial
coefficient lists,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf P=(P_4,I_2,P_5,I_3,P_6,I_4,J_2),
 \qquad P_5=I_3=0.
\end{equation}
The subscripts indicate degree in the relative tangent field $y$.
The displayed powers of $b$ are not part of these lists: their
contribution to the action is
\begin{equation}
 bP_4+I_2+bP_6+I_4+b^{-1}J_2,
\end{equation}
with the prescribed sums over labels and their masks.  Each entry
of $\mathbf P$ is a coefficient list, not a single scalar coupling.
The coefficient operation defines a deterministic map
$\mathbf P'=\mathcal C_h(\mathbf P)$, independent of the precise
$b$ once its displayed powers have been sorted.  It updates all
these lists together.  Starting from the nearest-neighbor action,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf P^{(0)}=0,\qquad
 \mathbf P^{(h+1)}=\mathcal C_h(\mathbf P^{(h)}).
\end{equation}
Blocking generates nonzero polynomial terms even from this zero
initial list.
Write $\alpha_h,\kappa_h$ for the increments evaluated on this orbit.
After $h=N-j$ completed steps the perturbative prediction is
\begin{equation}
 b_{j-1}^{\rm pert}=b_j+\alpha_{N-j}+\frac{\kappa_{N-j}}{b_j}.
\end{equation}
The omitted connected terms, cutoffs and localization corrections
produce a new regular remainder and covering terms.  Subtracting
the aligned value of each regular remainder changes the bulk
scalar; subtracting its affine Hessian gives a further kinetic
increment $\Delta_j$.  This scalar is distinct from the square
defect $\Delta(\xi)$ in \eqref{eq:delta}.  The exact construction proves
\begin{equation}
 b_{j-1}=b_j+\alpha_{N-j}+\frac{\kappa_{N-j}}{b_j}+\Delta_j,
 \qquad |\Delta_j|\le C_LH_j^{-1.05}.
 \label{eq:pert-exact-recursion}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The geometric contribution}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:alpha-geometric}
Choose a reference block $Y_0$ with centre $x_0$.
For every fine site and coarse block centre set
\begin{equation}
 h_x=\frac{x_1-x_{0,1}}{L},\qquad
 h_Y=\frac{x_{Y,1}-x_{0,1}}{L},\qquad
 u_x=h_x-h_{[x]}.
 \label{eq:geom-affine-coordinates}
\end{equation}
Thus $h_Y$ has unit slope in the first coarse coordinate and
$h_x$ has slope $1/L$ in fine coordinates.  With $w_x=w_{Y_0}(x)$,
define the dimensionless within-block second moment
\begin{equation}
 \sigma_w^2=\sum_{x\in Y_0}w_xu_x^2,\qquad
 \sum_{x\in Y_0}w_x=1,\qquad
 \sum_{x\in Y_0}w_xu_x=0.
 \label{eq:geom-weight-moment}
\end{equation}
Then the geometric one-loop coefficient
$\alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}$ defined in
\eqref{eq:alpha-geom-definition} is
\begin{align}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}
 ={}&-\frac{2}{L^2}\sum_{x\in Y_0}C_{xx}
     -\frac12\sum_{\substack{x\in Y_0\\y\in\mathbb Z^2}}
            C_{xy}K_{xy}(h_x-h_y)^2 \nonumber\\
 &+a\sum_{x,y\in Y_0}w_xw_y C_{xy}
       \left(2\sigma_w^2-\frac{u_x^2+u_y^2}{2}\right).
 \label{eq:alpha-geom-kernel}
\end{align}
Using $K=C^{-1}-aQ^*Q$ gives the equivalent formula
\begin{align}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}
 ={}&-\frac{2}{L^2}\sum_{x\in Y_0}C_{xx}
     -\frac12\sum_{\substack{x\in Y_0\\y\in\mathbb Z^2}}
        C_{xy}(C^{-1})_{xy}(h_x-h_y)^2 \nonumber\\
 &+a\sum_{x,y\in Y_0}w_xw_y C_{xy}
                         (2\sigma_w^2-u_xu_y).
 \label{eq:alpha-geom-covariance}
\end{align}
This expression uses only $C$, its inverse, and the known weights and coordinates.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Use a slowly rotating
background along a great circle:
\begin{equation}
 \bar q_x(\varepsilon)
   =(\sin(\varepsilon h_x),0,\cos(\varepsilon h_x)),\qquad
 V_Y(\varepsilon)
   =(\sin(\varepsilon h_Y),0,\cos(\varepsilon h_Y)).
 \label{eq:geom-helical-background}
\end{equation}
Its linear tangent variation is the test function of
\eqref{eq:beta-affine-test}, with anchor $O=Y_0$.
The sine parametrization differs from
\eqref{eq:pert-external-field-family} only at cubic order, so it
gives the same quadratic coefficient.  All derivatives here are bulk coefficient
derivatives at $\varepsilon=0$; no nonperiodic affine field is
inserted into a fixed periodic spin integral.
The even block weights give
\begin{equation}
 (Q\bar q)_Y=\rho(\varepsilon)V_Y(\varepsilon),\qquad
 \rho(\varepsilon)
   =\sum_{x\in Y_0}w_x\cos(\varepsilon u_x)
   =1-\tfrac12\sigma_w^2\varepsilon^2+O(\varepsilon^4).
 \label{eq:geom-block-mean}
\end{equation}
In particular, the normalized block mean equals $V_Y$ for small
$\varepsilon$.  Translation invariance and symmetry of $K$ give
\begin{equation}
 (K\bar q)_x=k(\varepsilon)\bar q_x,\qquad
 k(\varepsilon)=K(\varepsilon e_1/L)
               =\varepsilon^2/L^2+O(\varepsilon^4).
\end{equation}
Hence this background is stationary, in the bulk, for the
constrained energy
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal S_V(q)
 =\tfrac12\langle q,Kq\rangle
       +a\sum_Y\bigl(1-V_Y\cdot\widehat m_Y(q)\bigr).
 \label{eq:geom-constrained-energy}
\end{equation}
The tree-level energy has quadratic coarse slope coefficient
$b/2$; the order-one correction to that coefficient is obtained
from the Gaussian determinant at this stationary background.

Use the orthonormal tangent vectors
\begin{equation}
 e_{\parallel,x}
   =(\cos(\varepsilon h_x),0,-\sin(\varepsilon h_x)),
 \qquad e_{\perp,x}=(0,1,0).
\end{equation}
In intrinsic coordinates centred at $\bar q(\varepsilon)$, the
Hessian splits into two scalar matrices:
\begin{align}
 (A_\parallel(\varepsilon))_{xy}
 ={}&K_{xy}\cos\bigl(\varepsilon(h_x-h_y)\bigr)
        -k(\varepsilon)\delta_{xy} \nonumber\\
 &+\frac{a}{\rho(\varepsilon)^2}
       \sum_Y w_Y(x)w_Y(y)
             \cos(\varepsilon u_x)\cos(\varepsilon u_y),\\
 (A_\perp(\varepsilon))_{xy}
 ={}&K_{xy}-k(\varepsilon)\delta_{xy}
       +\frac{a}{\rho(\varepsilon)^2}
             \sum_Yw_Y(x)w_Y(y).
 \label{eq:geom-two-hessians}
\end{align}
The kinetic diagonal term comes from differentiating the sphere
constraint.  For the observation term, the Hessian with respect
to its mean $m=\rho V$ is
$a\rho^{-2}(\Id-VV^*)$; its first derivative vanishes there.
These facts give \eqref{eq:geom-two-hessians} directly.

Both Hessians equal $A=C^{-1}$ at $\varepsilon=0$ and have zero
first derivative.  Define the trace per coarse cell by
\begin{equation}
 \tr_{\mathrm{cell}} T=\sum_{x\in Y_0}T_{xx}.
\end{equation}
The background-dependent part of the one-loop action per coarse
site is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal G(\varepsilon)
 =\tfrac12\tr_{\mathrm{cell}}
       \bigl(\log A_\parallel(\varepsilon)
             +\log A_\perp(\varepsilon)-2\log A\bigr),
 \qquad
 \alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}=\mathcal G''(0).
 \label{eq:geom-logdet}
\end{equation}
This is the bulk trace density, equivalently obtained by Bloch
decomposition with one fine block as the cell.  The spherical
area density at the centre of each intrinsic chart is the same
constant $J(0)$, and the normalized observation has a
background-independent denominator.  They add no further
background-dependent term at this order.  The Jacobian and
coordinate-change contributions in the earlier vertex calculation
are thereby included in this intrinsic Hessian calculation.

Writing
$A_\parallel(\varepsilon)+A_\perp(\varepsilon)
 =2A+\varepsilon^2\mathsf B_{\mathrm{geom}}+O(\varepsilon^4)$ gives
\begin{align}
 (\mathsf B_{\mathrm{geom}})_{xy}
 ={}&-\tfrac12K_{xy}(h_x-h_y)^2
       -\frac{2}{L^2}\delta_{xy}\nonumber\\
 &+a\sum_Y w_Y(x)w_Y(y)
       \left(2\sigma_w^2-\frac{u_x^2+u_y^2}{2}\right),\\
 \alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}&=\tr_{\mathrm{cell}}(C\mathsf B_{\mathrm{geom}}).
\end{align}
Expanding this trace proves \eqref{eq:alpha-geom-kernel}.
Finally, for two sites in the same block,
$h_x-h_y=u_x-u_y$; substituting
$K=C^{-1}-aQ^*Q$ combines the observation terms into
$2\sigma_w^2-u_xu_y$ and proves
\eqref{eq:alpha-geom-covariance}.
\end{proof}




\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:geom-leading-log}
In the setting of Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-covariance-estimates},
let $\alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}$ be the coefficient defined in
Lemma~\ref{lem:alpha-geometric}.
Then
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}
   =-(n-2)\frac{\log L}{2\pi}+O(1),
 \label{eq:geom-leading-log}
\end{equation}
where $n=3$ since we are considering the $O(3)$ model.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The square-block coordinates in \eqref{eq:geom-affine-coordinates}
satisfy $|u_x|\le u_*:=1/2$, independently of $L$ and the history.
For $S^{n-1}$ there is one tangent direction parallel to the
helical background and $n-2$ perpendicular tangent directions.
Thus the logarithmic determinant in \eqref{eq:geom-logdet}
contains $\log A_\parallel+(n-2)\log A_\perp-(n-1)\log A$.
In \eqref{eq:alpha-geom-kernel}, the diagonal coefficient $-2$
is consequently replaced by $-(n-1)$ and $2\sigma_w^2$ by
$(n-1)\sigma_w^2$; the term containing
$K_{xy}(h_x-h_y)^2$ is unchanged.
Translation invariance and the normalization
$K(p)=|p|^2+O(|p|^4)$ imply the exact moment identity
\begin{equation}
 \sum_y K_{xy}(x_1-y_1)^2
 =-\partial_{p_1}^2K(0)=-2.
 \label{eq:geom-kinetic-moment}
\end{equation}
In the second term of \eqref{eq:alpha-geom-kernel}, use
$h_x-h_y=(x_1-y_1)/L$ and split
$C_{xy}=C_{xx}+(C_{xy}-C_{xx})$.  The part containing $C_{xx}$
is $+\overline C_L$, cancelling one of the $n-1$ diagonal
contributions.  Thus, exactly,
\begin{align}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}
   &=-(n-2)\overline C_L+R_{\mathrm{kin}}+R_{\mathrm{obs}},
       \label{eq:geom-log-decomposition}\\
 R_{\mathrm{kin}}
   &=-\frac1{2L^2}\sum_{\substack{x\in Y_0\\y\in\mathbb Z^2}}
       K_{xy}(C_{xy}-C_{xx})(x_1-y_1)^2,\\
 R_{\mathrm{obs}}
   &=a\sum_{x,y\in Y_0}w_xw_yC_{xy}
          \left((n-1)\sigma_w^2-\frac{u_x^2+u_y^2}{2}\right).
\end{align}
The three assumed bounds give
\begin{equation}
 |R_{\mathrm{kin}}|\le\frac M2,\qquad
 |R_{\mathrm{obs}}|\le na u_*^2M,
\end{equation}
since $\sigma_w^2\le u_*^2$.  Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \left|\alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}+(n-2)\frac{\log L}{2\pi}\right|
 \le \left(n-\frac32+na u_*^2\right)M,
\end{equation}
which proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The $O(1)$ remainder contains nonzero finite contributions:
at empty history, $K=d^*d$ gives
$R_{\mathrm{kin}}=-1/4+O(L^{-2})$.
For an admissible smooth averaging profile, numerical evaluation
also indicates a nonzero limit of the total remainder,
approximately $-0.54044$ when $a=1$.
Thus an improvement to $o(1)$ would require a cancellation
not established by the lemma; nonvanishing of the total limit
for this example is supported numerically, not proved here.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Perturbative contribution}

 \begin{lemma}
\label{lem:alpha-quartic-transfer}
The coefficient $\alpha_{\mathrm{pert}}$ is determined by a single
Gaussian contraction of $P_4$:
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_{\mathrm{pert}}=\alpha_{P_4}
             =\mathfrak a\bigl((F_{2,0;O}^{P_4})_O\bigr)
             =2F_{2,0;O}^{P_4}(n;\eta^{(O)}).
 \label{eq:alpha-quartic-transfer}
\end{equation}
Here $F_{2,0;O}^{P_4}$ is the quadratic output density given
explicitly in \eqref{eq:alpha-quartic-covariance}, $n=V_O$,
and $\eta^{(O)}$ is the rank-one affine test function of
\eqref{eq:beta-affine-test}.  The coefficient is independent
of $O$ and $n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The Gaussian completion
in \eqref{eq:Gauss-var} gives the linear harmonic extension
\begin{equation}
 H=aCQ^*,\qquad C=(K+aQ^*Q)^{-1},\qquad
 \psi=H\eta+Z^\top.
 \label{eq:alpha-harmonic-extension}
\end{equation}
Here $n=V_O$, $\psi$ is the rescaled physical fine tangent field, and
$Z^\top=\proj_{n^\perp}Z$ has covariance $C$ in each of its two
tangent components.  The mean $H\eta$ includes the variation of
$T_x=V_{[x]}$; it is not the coordinate shift $\mu$ alone.

Let $P_4^{\mathrm{tan}}(\psi)$ be the leading homogeneous quartic
part of the incoming $P_4$.  It is obtained from the polynomials
in \eqref{eq:quartic-anchor-polynomial} by replacing every
relative tangent argument at a fine anchor $z$ by
$\psi_{z+r}-\psi_z$.  All masks equal one in this formal
calculation.  Since $b=g^{-2}$, this term enters the action at
order $g^2$.  Its quadratic output is obtained by pairing exactly
two fluctuation factors.  Define that output density by
\begin{equation}
 F_{2,0;O}^{P_4}(n;\eta)
 =\left[
   \left.\frac12\sum_{x,y}C_{xy}\sum_{a=1}^2
       \frac{\partial^2P_4^{\mathrm{tan}}(\psi)}
            {\partial\psi_x^a\,\partial\psi_y^a}
   \right|_{\psi=H\eta}
  \right]_O.
 \label{eq:alpha-quartic-covariance}
\end{equation}
The factor $1/2$ is the Gaussian contraction factor.  With no
contraction the output has degree four; with two contractions it
is a scalar.  Thus \eqref{eq:alpha-quartic-covariance} is exactly
the anchored degree-two part of
$\E_C P_4^{\mathrm{tan}}(H\eta+Z^\top)$, with $\Tay_2$ understood
as in \eqref{eq:kinetic-loc-taylor}.

For example, consider the quartic monomial
$(v_1\cdot v_2)(v_3\cdot v_4)$ in
\eqref{eq:quartic-anchor-polynomial}, with $x_i=z+r_i$ and
$v_i=\psi_{x_i}-\psi_z$.  Define its mean differences and
fluctuation covariances by
\begin{equation}
 m_i=(H\eta)_{x_i}-(H\eta)_z,\qquad
 C^{(z)}_{ij}=C_{x_i x_j}-C_{x_i z}-C_{z x_j}+C_{zz}.
\end{equation}
At fixed external anchor spin $n$, its degree-two part in
$\eta$, before distributing the contribution among output
anchors, is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \Tay_2\E_C[(v_1\cdot v_2)(v_3\cdot v_4)]
 ={}&2C^{(z)}_{12}(m_3\cdot m_4)+2C^{(z)}_{34}(m_1\cdot m_2)\\
 &+C^{(z)}_{13}(m_2\cdot m_4)+C^{(z)}_{14}(m_2\cdot m_3)\\
 &+C^{(z)}_{23}(m_1\cdot m_4)+C^{(z)}_{24}(m_1\cdot m_3).
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The factors $2$ count the two tangent components.  Multiply by
the incoming monomial coefficient, sum over its fine anchors
and displacements, and apply $[\cdot]_O$ to obtain its contribution
to \eqref{eq:alpha-quartic-covariance}.  The other two tensor
pairings in \eqref{eq:quartic-anchor-polynomial} follow by
permuting the indices.

The Gaussian average of the incoming $I_2$ has quadratic part
$I_2^{\mathrm{tan}}(H\eta)$; its contraction is a scalar.
Its kinetic coefficient is zero.  The harmonic extension maps affine
coarse fields to affine fine fields, on which the compensated
quadratic packets have zero affine Hessian; see
\cite[Section~5.5]{OpenAI-O3}.  The other incoming slots start at
order $g^4$ and hence do not enter $\alpha$.

Thus $\alpha_{\mathrm{pert}}=\alpha_{P_4}$.  Applying
\eqref{eq:beta-affine-test} to the quadratic output gives the
last equality in \eqref{eq:alpha-quartic-transfer}.
Independence of $O$ and $n$ follows from
Proposition~\ref{prop:kinetic-coefficient-symmetries}.
\end{proof}


\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:pert-uniform-bound}
In the setting of Lemma~\ref{lem:alpha-quartic-transfer}, write
the incoming quartic polynomial in the pairing basis as
\begin{equation}
 P_4^{\mathrm{tan}}(\psi)
 =\sum_z\sum_\lambda a_{z,\lambda}
       (v_1\cdot v_2)(v_3\cdot v_4),
 \qquad v_i=\psi_{z+r_i}-\psi_z.
 \label{eq:pert-quartic-pairing-list}
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda$ records the displacements and the pairing;
the other pairings are represented by permuting the slots.
Assume translation covariance under coarse lattice translations
and that the harmonic extension of the rank-one affine test
function in \eqref{eq:beta-affine-test} has fine-scale gradient
$e\otimes e_1/L$, where $e$ is a unit tangent vector.
Assume the covariance increment bound
\eqref{eq:free-covariance-increments} with constant $M$,
and suppose that, for a fixed $\sigma>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \|P_4\|_\sigma\le M_4,
 \label{eq:pert-uniform-assumptions}
\end{equation}
where the norm is the coefficient-list norm
\eqref{eq:canonical-coefficient-norm} with $A_4=P_4$,
and $\sigma$, $M$ and $M_4$ are independent of $L$ and the
histories considered.
Then
\begin{equation}
 |\alpha_{\mathrm{pert}}|\le 16c_\sigma MM_4,
 \label{eq:pert-uniform-bound}
\end{equation}
where $c_\sigma$ depends only on $\sigma$.
In particular, $\alpha_{\mathrm{pert}}=O(1)$ uniformly in
$L$ and the history under these assumptions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
First, the norm assumption implies the fourth displacement
moment bound needed below.  Indeed, for
$S_\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^4|r_i|_1$, the arithmetic--geometric
mean inequality gives
\begin{equation}
 \prod_{i=1}^4|r_i|
 \le (S_\lambda/4)^4
 \le c_\sigma W_\sigma(1+S_\lambda),
 \qquad
 c_\sigma:=\sup_{t\ge0}
      \frac{(t/4)^4}{e^{\sigma(1+t)}(2+t)^2}<\infty.
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal M_4:=\sup_z\sum_\lambda |a_{z,\lambda}|
                          \prod_{i=1}^4|r_i|
 \le c_\sigma\|P_4\|_\sigma
 \le c_\sigma M_4.
 \label{eq:pert-fourth-moment}
\end{equation}
The affine-extension assumption gives
\begin{equation}
 |m_i|=\left|(H\eta)_{z+r_i}-(H\eta)_z\right|
       \le \frac{|r_i|}{L}.
\end{equation}
The six terms in the single-contraction formula in the proof
of Lemma~\ref{lem:alpha-quartic-transfer} have numerical
coefficients $2,2,1,1,1,1$.  The absolute quadratic contribution
of one monomial, including its coefficient, is consequently
at most
\begin{equation}
 \frac{8M}{L^2}|a_{z,\lambda}|
                     \prod_{i=1}^4|r_i|.
\end{equation}
There are $L^2$ fine anchors per coarse block.  The assignment
to output anchors preserves the sum and has nonnegative
weights summing to one for each contribution.  Translation
covariance therefore bounds the absolute assigned density
per coarse block by $8M\mathcal M_4$.  This can equivalently be seen
by summing over one translation cell before distributing
the contributions.  All resulting quadratic terms depend
on differences of the affine field, so changing its anchor
does not change this evaluation.  Finally,
\eqref{eq:alpha-quartic-transfer} supplies the factor $2$
from affine extraction and proves
\eqref{eq:pert-uniform-bound} using \eqref{eq:pert-fourth-moment}.
\end{proof}

The covariance in \eqref{eq:free-covariance-increments} is a
double difference, which cancels any common additive
logarithmic part.  The incoming coefficient norm bound remains
an additional hypothesis: the covariance bounds in
Proposition~\ref{prop:uniform-covariance-estimates} do not
control the incoming quartic coefficients.
The conclusion is boundedness, not vanishing, of
$\alpha_{\mathrm{pert}}$.
