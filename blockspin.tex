\section{Block spin transforms}
\label{sec:block-gauss}

This explains roughly Section~3.2.
%
The lattice $\Lambda$ has $n_{\rm fine}=|\Lambda|$ sites, $E$ oriented edges, and $B$ blocks.
Fields on the lattice are denoted $\phi$ and block spin fields $V$.

\subsection{Nonlinear block spin transforms}

The preprints use `soft' block spin transforms defined in terms of a penalization parameter $a>0$.
For a block $Y \subset \Lambda$, the linear block average of $\phi: \Lambda \to \R$ is defined by
\begin{equation}
 (Q\phi)_Y=\sum_{x\in Y}w_Y(x)\phi_x,
 \qquad w_Y(x)\ge0,\qquad \sum_{x\in Y}w_Y(x)=1.
\end{equation}
The nonlinear block spin average $\widehat m_Y(q)$ of a spin configuration $q: \Lambda \to S^2$ is then defined by
\begin{equation} \label{eq:hatm}
  m_Y(q) = (Qq)_Y, \qquad \widehat m_Y(q) =
  \begin{cases}
      \frac{m_Y(q)}{|m_Y(q)|} & (|m_Y(q)|\geq \frac12)
      \\
      \dots
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The block spin transform of a measure on configurations $q: \Lambda \to S^2$ is done by inserting the following factor into the measure for $q$:
\begin{equation}
\propto \prod_{Y \in \Lambda'} e^{-\frac{a}{2}|V_Y-\widehat m_Y(q)|^2} \, d\omega(V_Y)
\end{equation}
and integrating over $q$. This defines a new measure on block configurations $V :\Lambda' \to S^2$ where $Y\in\Lambda'$ are the blocks of the original lattice $\Lambda$.


\subsection{Gaussian block spin transforms}

For Gaussian measures, the block spin transform is an explicit recursion for the Gaussian functional (called `precision' in the preprints),
when the linear block average $Q\phi$ is used instead of $\widehat m$.

The kinetic term (or precision matrix) of the initial Gaussian measure is given by the Laplacian on the lattice $\Lambda$ [O3, above (3.4)]:
\begin{equation}
  K_{\varnothing} = d^* d.
\end{equation}
For a precision parameter $a>0$, the next-scale kinetic term is defined by [O3, (3.4)]
\begin{equation}
K_{h+1} = a\Id-a^2Q(K_h+aQ^*Q)^{-1}Q^*.
\end{equation}
This choice is explained by the following well-known fact.

\begin{proposition}
For a given block spin field $V\in\R^B$, 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:Gauss-var}
 \frac12\langle V,K_{h+1}V\rangle
 =\min_{\phi\in\R^{n_{\rm fine}}} \mathcal E_V(\phi)
 =\min_{\phi\in\R^{n_{\rm fine}}}
 \left\{\frac12\langle\phi,K_h\phi\rangle
              +\frac a2\|V-Q\phi\|^2\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here $n_{\rm fine}$ is the number of fine sites and $B$ the number
of blocks.  The minimization is over unrestricted real fields.
%
In particular, for any $V \in \R^B$ with $\phi_V$ the minimizer of $\mathcal E_V$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi)
  &= \min \mathcal E_V + \frac12 \langle \phi-\phi_V, \operatorname{Hess} \mathcal E_V(0)(\phi-\phi_V)\rangle 
  \\
  &= \frac12 \langle V, K_{h+1} V\rangle + \frac12 \langle \phi-\phi_V, \operatorname{Hess} \mathcal E_V(0)(\phi-\phi_V)\rangle 
  .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Set $A=K_h+aQ^*Q$. For $a>0$, this matrix is positive definite whenever $\ker K_h\cap\ker Q=\{0\}$.
The positive gradient term in $K_h$ makes its kernel consist of constants, while
$Q$ preserves constants, so this condition holds.
Expanding the energy gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_V(\phi)
 =\frac12\langle\phi,A\phi\rangle
  -a\langle\phi,Q^*V\rangle+\frac a2\|V\|^2.
\end{equation}
Its unique minimizer therefore satisfies
\begin{equation}
 A\phi_V=aQ^*V,\qquad
 \phi_V=a(K_h+aQ^*Q)^{-1}Q^*V.
\end{equation}
Completing the square yields
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_V(\phi)
 =\frac12\langle\phi-\phi_V,A(\phi-\phi_V)\rangle
  +\frac12\langle V,(a\Id-a^2QA^{-1}Q^*)V\rangle.
\end{equation}
The first term vanishes at $\phi=\phi_V$, giving the stated formula
for $K_{h+1}$.
\end{proof}


Note that $K_h$ is not finite range when $h \neq \varnothing$.
A sequence of block spin transformations is called \emph{history}
and the corresponding kinetic term $K_h$.


\subsection{Lifting the energy}

Let $E$ denote the set of directed edges and $d: \R^\Lambda \to \R^E$ and $d^*: \R^E \to \R^V$ be the discrete gradient and curl.

\begin{proposition}
The renormalized kinetic term can be written as 
\begin{equation}
 K_h
 =d^* \mathsf A_h^* \mathsf A_h d
 = c_0d^*d+d^*\ell_h^*\ell_hd,
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
\frac12\langle q,K_hq\rangle
=\frac{c_0}{2}\|dq\|^2+\frac12\|\ell_hdq\|^2,
\end{equation}
where the matrices $\ell_h$ and $\mathsf A_h$ are indexed by edges:
\begin{equation}
  \ell_h : \R^E \to \R^E,
  \qquad
  \mathsf A_h = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{c_0} I \\\ \ell_h \end{pmatrix} : \R^E \to \R^{E\times E}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By [O3, Section~4.2], $cD\le K_h\le CD$ and $K_h(p)-D(p)=O(|p|^4)$ in Fourier space, where
$d(p)=\binom{e^{ip_1}-1}{e^{ip_2}-1}$ and $D=d^*d$.
There is $B_h^{\rm raw}=I+O(|p|^2)$ with $K_h=d^*B_h^{\rm raw}d$.
Writing $\mathsf c=(-d_2,d_1)$, we have $\mathsf c d=0$, so
\begin{equation}
 B_h=B_h^{\rm raw}+\lambda_0\mathsf c^*\mathsf c
 \quad\text{satisfies}\quad d^*B_hd=K_h.
\end{equation}
One fixed sufficiently large $\lambda_0$ makes $B_h$ uniformly
positive: near $p=0$ the raw lift is close to $I$; away from $p=0$,
ellipticity controls the longitudinal entry and the added term
controls the transverse entry, including the mixed term by a Schur
complement.  The bounds are uniform in the history.

To use the preprint's particular positive completion [O3, Section~4.3],
make the further modification
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde B_h^{-1}
 =B_h^{-1}+\lambda_1 B_h^{-1}\mathsf c^*\mathsf c B_h^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Since $\widetilde B_h^{-1}B_hd=d$, this still gives
$\widetilde B_hd=B_hd$ and $d^*\widetilde B_hd=K_h$.
Choose $c_0>0$ below the uniform lower bound of $\widetilde B_h$ and set
\begin{equation}
 \ell_h=(\widetilde B_h-c_0I)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Then $\mathsf A_h^*\mathsf A_h=c_0I+\ell_h^*\ell_h
=\widetilde B_h$, proving the decomposition and its quadratic-form
identity.  The uniform analyticity and strict positivity imply that
$\ell_h$ has an exponentially decaying kernel.  At empty history the
construction retains $\widetilde B_{\varnothing}=I$.
\end{proof}
There is redundancy between $c_0$ and $\ell_h$.
Initially, for $0<c_0<1$,
\begin{equation}
 K_{\varnothing}=d^*d,
 \qquad \ell_{\varnothing}=\sqrt{1-c_0}\,\Id.
\end{equation}
%
The constant $c_0>0$ is fixed (uniformly over histories) to reserve an explicit positive nearest-neighbour term for local energy estimates.
The operator $\ell_h$ has exponential tail.
Later $\ell_s$ is a spatially truncated version of $\ell_h$ used in the finite range integration.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:D0}
Define $D_0: \R^{n_{\rm fine}} \to \R^{E \times E \times B}$ by
\begin{equation}
  D_0
  =\begin{pmatrix}
       \sqrt{c_0}\,d\\ \ell_s d\\ -\sqrt a\,Q
  \end{pmatrix}
  =\begin{pmatrix}
       \mathsf A_s d\\ -\sqrt a\,Q
  \end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 \mathsf A_s=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{c_0}\Id\\\ell_s\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
%where
%\begin{equation}
% d,\ell_s d:\R^{n_{\rm fine}}\longrightarrow\R^E,
% \qquad Q:\R^{n_{\rm fine}}\longrightarrow\R^B.
%\end{equation}
Then the Hessian of the variational functional $\mathcal E_V(\phi)$ in \eqref{eq:Gauss-var} can be written as
\begin{equation}
 \|D_0\phi\|^2
 = \langle \phi, D_0^* D_0 \phi \rangle
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi)- \mathcal E_V(\phi_V)
  = \frac12 \|D_0\phi +c_V - s_V\|^2,
\end{equation}
where $\phi_V$ is the minimizer of $\mathcal E_V$ and, for some $\nu_1,\nu_2,w$,
\begin{equation}
  c_V = \begin{pmatrix}
  0 \\ 0 \\ \sqrt{a}V
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  s_V
  = \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
  .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Hessian of the variational functional $\mathcal E_V(\phi)$ in \eqref{eq:Gauss-var} can be written as
\begin{equation}
 \|D_0\phi\|^2
 = \langle \phi, D_0^* D_0 \phi \rangle
 =c_0\sum_e|(d\phi)_e|^2
  +\sum_e|(\ell_s d\phi)_e|^2
  +a\sum_Y|(Q\phi)_Y|^2
\end{equation}
and the full Gaussian energy is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi) = \frac12 \|D_0\phi + c_V\|^2 ,
  \qquad
  c_V = \begin{pmatrix}
    0 \\ 0 \\ \sqrt{a} V 
  \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let $\phi_V = \operatorname{argmin} \mathcal E_V(\phi)$. Then 
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi_V + \eta) = \frac12 \|D_0\eta + s_V\|^2  = \frac12 \|D_0\eta\|^2 + \frac12 \|s_V\|^2,
  \qquad
  s_V
  = D_0\phi_V + c_V
  = \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
since $D^*s_V=0$. In other words,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi)- \mathcal E_V(\phi_V)
  = \frac12 \|D_0\phi - D_0\phi_V\|^2
  = \frac12 \|D_0\phi +c_V - s_V\|^2
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\section{Nonlinear background field coordinates}
\label{sec:background-field}

This explains Section~5.2.

\subsection{Fluctuation integral}

Throughout this section, $S\subset \Lambda$ are the nonfrozen fine sites (small field region) with
 one spin $q_x \in S^2$ integrated at each site $x\in S$. 
Their number is $n=|S|$.
For each site $x\in S$, there is a background field of reference spins $T_x\in  S^2$
which are held fixed. We consider the integral
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I=
 \int_{(S^2)^S} H(q)
    \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q)|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
    \prod_{x\in S}d\omega(q_x),
 \label{eq:spin-integral}
\end{equation}
where $|R(q)|^2$ is a reference energy and $d\omega$ the normalized measure on $S^2$.
The function $H(q)$ contains nonlocal contributions not explicitly tracked in $|R(q)|^2$.


\subsection{Reference energy}

The precise form of the reference energy $|R(q)|^2$ is unimportant for the algebraic manipulations
in the subsequent subsections, but it motivated their choice.
$|R(q)|^2$ is a nonlinear version of the Gaussian energy with soft block spin condition,
defined in the last section:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal E_V(\phi) - \mathcal E_V(\phi_V) = \frac12 \|D_0\phi+c_V-s_V\|^2.
\end{equation}
More precisely, first restrict $D_0: \R^{n} \to \R^{E \times E \times B}$ 
to nonfrozen sites $S \subset \Lambda$ and `selected' components of the range $R\subset E\times E \times B$
(selecting small gradients or so):
\begin{equation}
D = D_0|_{S\times R}.
\end{equation}
%
For the purpose of the discussion, we ignore this restriction; it does not change much.
Thus, up to dropping rows and columns, in Proposition~\ref{prop:D0}, it was shown that
\begin{equation}
D\phi +c_V -s_V = \begin{pmatrix}
  \sqrt{c_0} d\phi\\
  \ell_s d\phi\\
  \sqrt{a}V -\sqrt{a}Q\phi
\end{pmatrix}
-s_V,
  \qquad
  s_V = \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
.
\end{equation}
%
For $q: \R^n  \to S^2$, define $R(q)$ as a nonlinear version of this:
\begin{equation}
  R(q) = \begin{pmatrix}
  \sqrt{c_0} dq\\
  \ell_s dq\\
  \sqrt{a}(V-\widehat m(q))
  \end{pmatrix}
  - \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ w
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where we recall that $\widehat m_Y(q)$ is the nonlinear block average \eqref{eq:hatm}.

Concretely, the quantity $\tfrac12|R(q)|^2$ arises as follows.
The renormalized density representation \eqref{eq:density} describes the full density before or after that
step.  The connection is obtained by extracting the coarse kinetic
energy from the fine kinetic energy plus the block-spin observation
term.  Write \eqref{eq:density} as
\begin{equation}
 \rho_j(q)=e^{\mathfrak v_j|\Lambda_j|}
          e^{-b\mathcal E_{h,t}(q)}G_j(q).
\end{equation}
The block transformation integrates this against the block spin
kernel whose exponential part is
\begin{equation}
 e^{-\frac{ba}{2}\|V-\widehat m(q)\|^2}.
\end{equation}
Then the leading part of the energy decomposition has the following form:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:cEqV}
 \mathcal E_{h,t}(q)
 +\frac a2\|V-\widehat m(q)\|^2
 =\mathcal E_{h',t'}(V)
  +\frac12|R(q;V)|^2+\mathcal L(q,V).
\end{equation}
Here $h',t'$ are the next-scale history and large field scale, $R(q;V)$
denotes the selected residual rows defined above and $\mathcal L(q,V)$ collects the remainder.

\subsection{Intrinsic tangent coordinates}

Consider a single site and reference spin $T \in S^2$.
For $u\in T^\perp$,
\begin{equation}
 \Exp_T u=\cos|u|\,T+\frac{\sin|u|}{|u|}u,
 \qquad \Exp_T0=T.
\end{equation}
The restriction $|u|<\pi$ parametrizes $S^2\setminus\{-T\}$ and the omitted point has area zero.
Note
\begin{equation}
 d\omega(\Exp_Tu)=J(u)\,d^2u,
 \qquad J(u)=\frac1{4\pi}\frac{\sin|u|}{|u|},
 \qquad J(0)=\frac1{4\pi}.
 \label{eq:area}
\end{equation}
In tangent coordinates, 
$q(u)=(\Exp_{T_x}u_x)_{x\in S}$ and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal U=\prod_{x\in S}
       \{u_x\in T_x^\perp:|u_x|<\pi\}.
\end{equation}
The spherical area formula gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I=
 \int_{\mathcal U} H(q(u))
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q(u))|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \prod_{x\in S}\bigl[J(u_x)\,d^2u_x\bigr].
\end{equation}
Here $d^2u_x$ is Euclidean area measure on $T_x^\perp$.
This integral has $2n$ real coordinates.

\subsection{Auxiliary normal coordinate}

Introduce one real variable $v_x\in\R$ at each nonfrozen site.
Since $A=D^*D$ is positive definite, the elementary
Gaussian integral gives
\begin{equation}
 1=\frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
       \int_{\R^n}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{v^*Av}{2g^2}\right\}d^nv.
 \label{eq:scalar-one}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the inserted probability law is a centered scalar
Gaussian with covariance $g^2A^{-1}$.  Its energy is
$v^*Av=|Dv|^2$.  No cutoff is imposed on $v$, and
the inserted variables are initially independent of the spin variables.
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathcal I={}&\frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 \int_{\R^n}\int_{\mathcal U} H(q(u))\\
 &\quad\times
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q(u))|^2+|Dv|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \prod_{x\in S}\bigl[J(u_x)\,d^2u_x\, dv_x\bigr].
 \label{eq:uv-integral}
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
There are now $2n+n=3n$ real integration coordinates.
The purpose of the extra coordinate is
to complete each tangent plane to the ambient space via
\begin{equation}
\xi_x=u_x+T_xv_x
.
\end{equation}
Thus the scalar $v_x$ is an auxiliary normal coordinate, not a new physical spin component.

\subsection{Combining the coordinates into an ambient field}

Conversely, for $\xi=(\xi_x)_{x\in S}\in(\R^3)^S$, define the tangential and normal directions
\begin{equation}
 u_x(\xi)=\xi_x-(T_x\cdot\xi_x)T_x,
 \qquad
 v_x(\xi)=T_x\cdot\xi_x
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
 q_x(\xi)=\Exp_{T_x}u_x(\xi).
\end{equation}
The change of variables $(u_x,v_x) \mapsto \xi_x=u_x+T_xv_x$ has Jacobian one.
Thus
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal I=
 \frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 \int_{\R^{3n}} H(q(\xi))\,\mathcal J(\xi)
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|R(q(\xi))|^2+|Dv(\xi)|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \,d^{3n}\xi ,
\end{equation}
where the chart factor is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal J(\xi)=\prod_{x\in S} J(u_x(\xi)) 1_{|u_x(\xi)| < \pi}.
 %j(u)=
 %\begin{cases}
 %  \sin|u|/|u|,& |u|<\pi,\\
 %  0,& |u|\ge\pi,
 %\end{cases}
 %\qquad j(0)=1.
\end{equation}
%The factor $J(0)^n\mathcal J(\xi) = (4\pi)^{-n} \mathcal J(\xi)$ supplies the spherical
%Jacobian and the principal-chart restriction, while
%$(\det A)^{1/2}/(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}$ normalizes the auxiliary normal Gaussian.

\subsection{Gaussian coordinates}

Recall that $D: (\R^{3})^n \to (\R^3)^m$ acts on the nonfrozen coordinates.
Let $F=(F_i)_{i=1}^m\in(\R^3)^m$ be a fixed vector in the range so that
\begin{equation}
 (D\xi+F)_i=\sum_{x\in S}D_{ix}\xi_x+F_i.
\end{equation}
%
Define 
\begin{equation}
 \Delta(\xi)=|R(q(\xi))|^2+|Dv(\xi)|^2-|D\xi+F|^2.
 \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
Note that $R(q(\xi))$ is defined in terms of the physical sphere valued coordinates
whereas $D\xi+F$ is the affine ambient expression chosen for the Gaussian computation.
%
Clearly,
\begin{equation}
 e^{-(|R(q(\xi))|^2+|Dv|^2)/(2g^2)}
 =e^{-|D\xi+F|^2/(2g^2)}e^{-\Delta(\xi)/(2g^2)}.
\end{equation}
The first term on the right-hand side is a Gaussian density with
covariance $g^2A^{-1}$ where $A=D^* D$.


\subsection{Gaussian expectation identity}

It will be useful to choose a Gaussian reference measure with more general covariance $g^2C$ than $g^2 A^{-1}$.
Choose any symmetric positive definite $n\times n$ matrix $C$
and any $\mu\in(\R^3)^S$.
Let $Z\in(\R^3)^S$ have the normalized Gaussian density with
\begin{equation}
\E[Z_x^\alpha Z_y^\beta]=C_{xy}\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
\end{equation}
%
The vector $\mu\in(\R^3)^S$ is an arbitrary fixed shift in
the Gaussian coordinates
\begin{equation}
 \xi=\mu+gZ,\qquad Z\sim N(0,C\otimes I_3).
\end{equation}
The covariance $C$ has not been determined yet, but it will have finite range.

\begin{remark}
The unique minimizer of the Gaussian action
\begin{equation}
\mathcal E_F(\xi)=
\frac12|D\xi+F|^2
\end{equation}
is 
\begin{equation}
\xi = \mu_*=-A^{-1}D^*F.
\end{equation}
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
\begin{equation}
 \nabla_\xi\frac12|D\xi+F|^2=A\xi+D^*F,
\end{equation}
so positive definiteness of $A$ makes $\mu_*$ the unique minimizer. 
\end{proof}

This above choice $\mu=\mu_*$ gives $h=0$ in the identity below, eliminating the term linear in $Z$ in the exponent in the following proposition.
The general identity below permits other choices. The preprint uses a finite range approximation
\begin{equation}
 \mu_s=-\Pi_0^*D^*F,
\end{equation}
related to the choice $C=C_s$ where
\begin{equation}
  C_s = \frac12 (\Pi_0+\Pi_0^*),
\end{equation}
where the finite range condition is enforced by
\begin{equation}
  (\Pi_{a_0})_{yx} = 1_{y\in\Omega_x} (A_{\Omega_x} +a_0)^{-1}_{yx}.
\end{equation}
For this choice, $h=A\mu_s+D^*F$ need not vanish;
its contribution is retained explicitly.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:background-expect}
Given arbirary $F$, for any $C$ and $\mu$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal I={}&
 \kappa_g^n(\det A)^{1/2}(\det C)^{3/2}
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|r|^2}{2g^2}\right\}
 \\
 &\E_{\gamma_C}\left[
 H(q(\mu+gZ))\,\mathcal J(\mu+gZ)
 \exp\!\left\{
 -\frac{\Delta(\mu+gZ)}{2g^2}
 +\frac12 Z^*(C^{-1}-A)Z
 -g^{-1}(h,Z)
 \right\}\right],
 \label{eq:main}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where %$h^*Z=\sum_xh_x\cdot Z_x$ and
\begin{equation}
 r=D\mu+F,\qquad h=A\mu+D^*F,
 \qquad \kappa_g=2\pi g^2.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the above,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal I={}&
 \frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 \int_{\R^{3n}}H(q(\xi))\,\mathcal J(\xi)
 \exp\!\left\{-\frac{|D\xi+F|^2+\Delta(\xi)}{2g^2}\right\}d^{3n}\xi.
 \label{eq:lebesgue}
\end{align}
Now set $\xi=\mu+gZ$, whose Jacobian is $g^{3n}$.
Ordinary multiplication of squares gives
\begin{equation}
 |D(\mu+gZ)+F|^2
 =|r|^2+2g\,h^*Z+g^2Z^*AZ.
\end{equation}
The constant is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{(\det A)^{1/2}}{(2\pi g^2)^{n/2}}
 g^{3n}(2\pi)^{3n/2}(\det C)^{3/2}
 =\kappa_g^n(\det A)^{1/2}(\det C)^{3/2}.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\section{Parametrization of the renormalized action}

The renormalized action or more precisely its exponential (called `retained density') is parametrized in [O3, (3.29)].
The density is relative to product normalized spherical area measure $\prod_xd\omega(q_x)$.
Its representation is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 \rho_j(q)={}&
 e^{\mathfrak v|\Lambda_j|-b\mathcal E_{h,t}(q)}\,
 \exp\!\left\{
 -\sum_{\alpha}
       1_{X_\alpha}(q)\,\mathcal P_\alpha(q)
 -\sum_X1_X(q)f_X(q)\right\}\,\Xi(q).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
The different terms describe:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathfrak v$ is the partition function renormalization (a constant);
\item $b \mathcal E_{h,t}$ is a regularized version of the kinetic energy. If all gradients of $q$
are small then
\begin{equation}
\mathcal E_{h,t}(q) = \frac12 \langle q, K_h q\rangle
\end{equation}
and $t$ parametrizes a cutoff scale for the size of gradients.

This term is marginal and has size $g^0$. Indeed, its prefactor $b\approx 1/g^2$ and fluctuations are of size $dq^2 \approx g^2$.

\item $\mathcal P_\alpha$ are irrelevant second- to sixth-order monomials, of formal orders $g^2$ and $g^4$.
\item $f$ are the remaining irrelevant terms of size $\approx g^{4.1}$ (smaller than second order in $g^2$). 
\item $\Xi$ are nonperturbative large field terms (smaller than $e^{-(\log g)^\epsilon}$).
\end{itemize}
%
Given a field scale parameter $t>0$:
\begin{itemize}
\item 
The large gradient region (set of bad edges) is defined by
\begin{equation}
 D(q)=\{e: |d_e q|>t\}.
 \label{eq:bad-edges}
\end{equation}
\item
The indicator function that $X$ is in the small gradient region (only has good edges) is
\begin{equation}
 1_X(q)=\mathbf1\{|d_eq| \le  t\text{ for every }e\in X\}
 =\mathbf1\{X\cap D(q) = \varnothing\}.
 \label{eq:mask}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}
 The parameter is chosen as $g_j \ll t \ll g_j^{1-\epsilon}$. Typical fluctuations should be of order $g_j$.

\subsection{Renormalized kinetic energy $\mathcal E$}

The renormalized kinetic term is defined by
\begin{equation}
\exp\big\{-b\mathcal E_{h,t}(q) \big\}.
\end{equation}
Given fixed $0<d_0<c_0/16$ and $R_* \gg 1$, for any large field scale $t>0$, define
\begin{align}
 S_t(r)&=\begin{cases}
 c_0r^2/2,&r\le t,\\
 d_0tr,&r>t,
 \end{cases}\\
 m_{e,t}(q)&=\mathbf1\!\left\{
 |dq_f|\le t\,e^{\operatorname{dist}(e,f)/R_*}
                       \text{ for every edge }f\right\},
\end{align}
and the regularized kinetic energy
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_{h,t}(q)
 =
 \sum_e S_t(|d_e(q)|)+
 \frac12\sum_em_e(q)|(\ell_hdq)_e|^2.
 \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
The constant $c_0$ and the edge operator $\ell_h$ are those from the Gaussian decomposition in Section~\ref{sec:block-gauss}:
\begin{equation}
 K_h=c_0d^*d+d^*\ell_h^*\ell_hd.
\end{equation}
If every edge is good, then $m_e=1$ for every $e$ and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_{h,t}(q)=\frac12\langle q,K_hq\rangle.
\end{equation}
Thus this is a nonlinear version of the Gaussian reference measure that we typically use in RG.

\subsection{High order irrelevant terms $f$}

The contribution is
\begin{equation}
  \exp\left\{-\sum_X 1_X(q) f_X(q) \right\}.
\end{equation}
The actitivies $f_X$ are defined for connected $X$ with support and have norms
\begin{equation}
  \|f\| = \sup_o \sum_{X \ni o} [f_X].
\end{equation}
The brackets denote the scaled local $C^k$ norm
\begin{equation}
 [f_X]_{k,j}
 =\sum_{r=0}^{k}
   \sup_{q\in\mathcal U_{X}}
   \sup_{v_1,\ldots,v_r}
   |D_{v_1,\ldots,v_r}f_X(q)|,
 \qquad 0\le k\le8.
 \label{eq:error-seminorm}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal U_X$ is a set of spins containing the support of $1_X$.
Writing $v\times$ for the skew-symmetric linear map
$w\mapsto v\times w$ on $\R^3$, the rotation derivatives are
\begin{equation}
 D_{v_1,\ldots,v_r}f_X(q)
 =\left.
   \partial_{\theta_1}\cdots\partial_{\theta_r}
   f_X\!\left(\left(
     \exp\!\left[\left(\sum_{a=1}^{r}\theta_av_{a,x}\right)
                      \times\right]q_x
   \right)_x\right)
   \right|_{\theta=0}.
 \label{eq:error-rotation-jets}
\end{equation}
The supremum runs over vector fields satisfying
\begin{equation}
 |v_{a,x}|\le t(1+\operatorname{dist}(x,o))^8.
 \label{eq:error-directions}
\end{equation}
The rotations keep the spins on $S^2$.
Each derivative is scaled by $t$, so schematically this norm resembles
$\sum_{r=0}^k t^r\|\nabla^r f_X\|_\infty$, with spatial weights
and derivatives adapted to the sphere.  The exact definition is
[O3, (3.20)--(3.22)].

\subsection{Nonperturbative terms $\Xi$}

The exact sum is
\begin{equation}
 \Xi(q)=\sum_{\substack{\Gamma\text{ a finite family of labels}\\
       P_\ell\cap P_{\ell'}=\varnothing\ (\ell\ne\ell')\\
       \bigsqcup_{\ell\in\Gamma}J_\ell=D(q)}}
                    \prod_{\ell\in\Gamma}k_\ell(q).
 \label{eq:covering}
\end{equation}
The polymer activities satisfy:
\begin{itemize}
  \item 
Each $k_\ell(q)$ is supported in the large field region:
\begin{equation}
 k_\ell(q)=0\quad\text{unless}\quad J_\ell\subset D(q).
\end{equation}
In particular, if $D(q)=\varnothing$ (there are no large fields), then $\Xi(q)=1$.
\item
The size of the $k_\ell$ is measured in $L^\infty$ rather than $C^8$:
\begin{equation}
 \|k\|
 = \sup_x\sum_{\ell:x\in P_\ell}
 e^{64s_\ell}\|k_\ell\|_\infty
 \label{eq:cover-bound}
\end{equation}
and eventually $\|k\| \le w=e^{-p^{1/4}}$.
\end{itemize} 

\subsection{Low order irrelevant terms $\mathcal P$}

These are the terms:
\begin{equation}
\exp\!\left\{
 -\sum_{\alpha}
       1_{X_\alpha}(q)\,\mathcal P_\alpha(q) \right\}
       .
\end{equation}
These are essentially irrelevant sixth order polynomials
written in tangent coordinates relative to a reference spin (part of $\alpha$).
They are defined in [O3, (3.13)--(3.19)]. 

\begin{definition}
The relative tangent projection of $q_x$ at an anchor $o$ is
\begin{equation}
 y_o(x)=q_x-(q_o\cdot q_x)q_o\in q_o^\perp.
 \label{eq:canonical-tangent}
\end{equation}
This is a projection, not the logarithmic coordinate $\Log_{q_o}q_x$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
An invariant tensor of degree $n$ is a multilinear map
$T:(\R^2)^n\to\R$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 T(Rv_1,\ldots,Rv_n)=T(v_1,\ldots,v_n)
 \qquad\text{for every }R\in O(2).
\end{equation}
The vectors $v_i$ represent tangent vectors in $q_o^\perp$ for a fixed anchor $o$.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
At degree two, a basis consists of the single tensor
\begin{equation}
 T(v_1,v_2)=v_1\cdot v_2,
 \qquad T_{ab}=\delta_{ab}.
\end{equation}
At degree four, a basis consists of the three pairings
\begin{align}
 T_{12|34}(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)
   &=(v_1\cdot v_2)(v_3\cdot v_4),\\
 T_{13|24}(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)
   &=(v_1\cdot v_3)(v_2\cdot v_4),\\
 T_{14|23}(v_1,v_2,v_3,v_4)
   &=(v_1\cdot v_4)(v_2\cdot v_3).
\end{align}
For instance, $(T_{12|34})_{abcd}=\delta_{ab}\delta_{cd}$.
%These are the tensor types in the quartic polynomial below.
At degree six, examples are
\begin{equation}
 (v_1\cdot v_2)(v_3\cdot v_4)(v_5\cdot v_6),
 \qquad
 (v_1\cdot v_3)(v_2\cdot v_5)(v_4\cdot v_6).
\end{equation}
All fifteen pairings span the degree-six invariant space.  In
dimension two they have linear relations, so a basis is obtained
by choosing a linearly independent subset.  Odd degrees have no
nonzero invariant tensors: applying $R=-I$ gives
\begin{equation}
 T(v_1,\ldots,v_n)=(-1)^nT(v_1,\ldots,v_n).
\end{equation}
The basis of tensor types is fixed once and for all.
\end{example}

Let $q_o\in S^2$ and let $T:(\R^2)^n\to\R$ be an
$O(2)$-invariant multilinear tensor.  For $v_1,\ldots,v_n\in q_o^\perp$
and any linear isometry $\iota:q_o^\perp\to\R^2$, the value
\begin{equation}
 T_{q_o}(v_1,\ldots,v_n):=T(\iota v_1,\ldots,\iota v_n)
\end{equation}
is independent of $\iota$.  Thus $T$ defines a multilinear tensor
on the tangent plane without choosing an orthonormal frame.

The corresponding global covariance rotates the anchor spin and
all tangent vectors simultaneously:
\begin{equation}
 T_{Rq_o}(Rv_1,\ldots,Rv_n)=T_{q_o}(v_1,\ldots,v_n),
 \qquad R\in O(3).
\end{equation}
Indeed, if $\iota$ is an orthonormal identification at $q_o$, then
$\iota R^{-1}$ is one at $Rq_o$.  For tangent vectors obtained
from physical spins, the same covariance follows from
\begin{equation}
 \proj_{(Rq_o)^\perp}(Rq_x)
 =R\proj_{q_o^\perp}q_x.
\end{equation}
Thus a simultaneous global transformation of the spin configuration
leaves each invariant polynomial unchanged.  Holding the anchor
spin fixed restricts this symmetry to its stabilizer,
\begin{equation}
 \{R\in O(3):Rq_o=q_o\}\cong O(2),
\end{equation}
which is the $O(2)$ acting on tangent vectors in the definition of $T_\alpha$. 

\begin{definition}
A monomial label 
\begin{equation}
\alpha = (o_\alpha,n_\alpha,\varepsilon_\alpha,a_\alpha,{\bf r}_\alpha,T_\alpha, X_\alpha, S_\alpha, R_\alpha,s_\alpha)
\end{equation}
specifies an anchor $o_\alpha$, a degree $n_\alpha$, a coefficient
$a_\alpha$, displacements $r_1^\alpha,\ldots,r_{n_\alpha}^\alpha\in\mathbb Z^2$, and an
$O(2)$-invariant multilinear tensor $T_\alpha$.  Writing $o=o_\alpha$,
its monomial is
\begin{equation}
 a_\alpha T_\alpha\bigl(y_o(o+r_1^\alpha),\ldots,y_o(o+r_{n_\alpha}^\alpha)\bigr).
\end{equation}
The tensors $T_\alpha$ are chosen from a fixed basis of the invariant tensor space.
The label also contains the following geometric data:
\begin{itemize}
\item the mask graph $X_\alpha$ is a connected graph
containing the anchor $o$ and the sites $o+r_i^\alpha$, with the
joining paths, enlargements, and complete record $R_\alpha$ prescribed by its construction;
%
\item the complete support $S_\alpha$ is a set of vertices containing every spin
argument and every site or edge contained in $X_\alpha$ and extended by the range of the finite-range covariances;
\item
the record $R_\alpha$ is a connected collection of cubes of radius
$\mathfrak m_j=\lceil(\log H_j)^2\rceil$ and joining paths covering
$S_\alpha$ and containing $X_\alpha$.  It retains path multiplicities
and, on a torus, displacements before reduction modulo the periods
\item its integer load $s_\alpha\ge1$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 |S_\alpha|\le C\mathfrak m_j^2s_\alpha,
 \qquad
 \operatorname{length}(R_\alpha)\le C\mathfrak m_js_\alpha,
\end{equation}
where $|S_\alpha|$ counts sites, including endpoints of queried edges.
The load need not be minimal.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

 A_n(q)=\sum_\alpha 1_{X_\alpha}(q)\,a_\alpha

 \|A_n\|_\sigma

\subsubsection{Example}

For example, the quartic polynomial anchored at $o$ can be written
explicitly using the three $O(2)$-invariant dot-product pairings.
Writing $\mathbf r=(r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4)$ and $y_i=y_o(o+r_i)$,
and displaying the polynomial without its graph masks, we have
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 P_{4,o}(y)=\sum_{\mathbf r\in(\mathbb Z^2)^4}\bigl[&
 a^{12|34}_{o;\mathbf r}(y_1\cdot y_2)(y_3\cdot y_4)\\
 &+a^{13|24}_{o;\mathbf r}(y_1\cdot y_3)(y_2\cdot y_4)\\
 &+a^{14|23}_{o;\mathbf r}(y_1\cdot y_4)(y_2\cdot y_3)\bigr].
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:quartic-anchor-polynomial}
\end{equation}
The $a^{ij|kl}_{o;\mathbf r}$ are the quartic coefficients, with
the action prefactor $b$ excluded.  In the retained representation,
each coefficient keeps its graph label and mask; these extra labels
are suppressed here.  Summing the masked terms over anchors gives
$P_4$, whose contribution to the action is $bP_4$.

These coefficients are generally not finite range.  The displacement
tuple ranges over $(\mathbb Z^2)^4$; terms with any $r_i=0$ vanish
because $y_o(o)=0$.  Instead of finite range, the coefficient norm
requires exponential spatial decay:
\begin{equation}
 \sup_o\sum_{\alpha:\,o(\alpha)=o}
 |a_\alpha|\,
 e^{\sigma(1+\sum_{i=1}^4|r_i^\alpha|_1)}
 \left(2+\sum_{i=1}^4|r_i^\alpha|_1\right)^2\le C.
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha$ includes the pairing type and all additional retained
labels of the quartic coefficient.  Interactions reaching far from
the anchor are therefore small but need not vanish.  The finite
range of the fluctuation covariance $C_s$ does not imply finite
range of these coefficients: their canonical perturbative calculation
uses full kernels.  On a finite torus the positions are folded
periodically, while the decay bounds use the lifted displacement
records.

The same polynomial can be expressed directly in the spin variables.
Since $|q_o|=1$, define
\begin{equation}
 G_o(x,z):=y_o(x)\cdot y_o(z)
 =q_x\cdot q_z-(q_o\cdot q_x)(q_o\cdot q_z).
\end{equation}
Writing $x_i=o+r_i$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 P_{4,o}(q)=\sum_{\mathbf r\in(\mathbb Z^2)^4}\bigl[&
 a^{12|34}_{o;\mathbf r}G_o(x_1,x_2)G_o(x_3,x_4)\\
 &+a^{13|24}_{o;\mathbf r}G_o(x_1,x_3)G_o(x_2,x_4)\\
 &+a^{14|23}_{o;\mathbf r}G_o(x_1,x_4)G_o(x_2,x_3)\bigr].
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:quartic-anchor-spins}
\end{equation}
The same graph masks are attached to the retained labels.
Global $O(3)$ invariance is explicit because only scalar products
of spins occur.  The word ``quartic'' refers to degree in the
relative tangent coordinates, not degree in all the ambient spin
variables $q$.

\end{equation}
The term $\mathcal L$ is the defect
between the linear and normalized block average in \eqref{eq:cEqV}.

\begin{proposition}[Symmetry]
\label{prop:spin-symmetry}
For globally $O(3)$-invariant input density,
the output density and the effective interaction $\mathcal W$
are invariant under global
$O(3)$ transformations of the spins.
The connected Taylor coefficients inherit this invariance. 
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
First consider an internal spin transformation $R\in O(3)$.
The block mean obeys $\widehat m(Rq)=R\widehat m(q)$ (with
the same tag if needed).  Hence the normalized
block spin kernel $\mathcal B(V,q)$ obeys
$\mathcal B(RV,Rq)=\mathcal B(V,q)$.  Invariance of the incoming
density and spherical measure gives, by $q=R\widetilde q$,
\begin{equation}
 \rho'(RV)
 =\int\rho(q)\mathcal B(RV,q)\,d\omega(q)
 =\int\rho(\widetilde q)\mathcal B(V,\widetilde q)
                      \,d\omega(\widetilde q)
 =\rho'(V).
\end{equation}
Here the integrals use product spherical measure and include the
tag average when needed.

The perturbative representation preserves the same symmetry.
Indeed, the intrinsic coordinates and tangent projections satisfy
\begin{equation}
 \Exp_{RT}(Ru)=R\Exp_T(u),\qquad
 \proj_{(RT)^\perp}(R\xi)=R\proj_{T^\perp}\xi.
\end{equation}
The projected affine rows are equivariant and the Gaussian covariance
is $C\otimes I_3$.  Thus $RZ$ has the same law as $Z$, and
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal V(RV,RZ)=\mathcal V(V,Z),\qquad
 \mathcal W(RV)=\mathcal W(V).
\end{equation}
Taking the connected expansion and each homogeneous Taylor part
preserves these identities.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Connected Wick contractions and power counting}

Write $\langle W_1;\ldots;W_m\rangle_c$ for a Gaussian cumulant. Then the expansion is
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W
 =\langle\mathcal V\rangle
  -\frac12\langle\mathcal V;\mathcal V\rangle_c
  +\frac16\langle\mathcal V;\mathcal V;\mathcal V\rangle_c
  -\frac1{24}\langle\mathcal V;\mathcal V;
                         \mathcal V;\mathcal V\rangle_c+\cdots.
 \label{eq:pert-cumulants}
\end{equation}
Assign order $g$ to both a relative output tangent $y$ and the fluctuation $gZ$.
For example, after setting
$y=g\eta$ and writing $\mathcal V=\sum_{r\ge1}g^r\mathcal V_r$, the
coefficients at orders two and four are
\begin{align}
 [g^2]\mathcal W
 &=\langle \mathcal V_2\rangle-\tfrac12\langle \mathcal V_1;\mathcal V_1\rangle_c,\\
 [g^4]\mathcal W
 &=\langle \mathcal V_4\rangle-\langle \mathcal V_1;\mathcal V_3\rangle_c
   -\tfrac12\langle \mathcal V_2;\mathcal V_2\rangle_c
   +\tfrac12\langle \mathcal V_1;\mathcal V_1;\mathcal V_2\rangle_c
   -\tfrac1{24}\langle \mathcal V_1;\mathcal V_1;\mathcal V_1;\mathcal V_1\rangle_c.
\end{align}

the neighbouring spins in relative tangent coordinates
\begin{equation}
 y_o(Y)=V_Y-(V_o\cdot V_Y)V_o.
\end{equation}
Holding $V_o$ fixed, expand that term around $y_o=0$.  After
extracting field-independent terms, organize the resulting Taylor
coefficients as follows:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal W_{\le4}
 =bF_{2,1}(y)+F_{2,0}(y)
   +bF_{4,1}(y)+F_{4,0}(y)+b^{-1}F_{4,-1}(y).
 \label{eq:pert-sorted}
\end{equation}
Here $F_{r,d}$ has field degree $r+2d$, so $g^{-2d}F_{r,d}$
has formal order $g^r$.  Thus the field degrees are $4,2,6,4,2$.
More precisely, after writing $y=g\eta$, homogeneity gives
\begin{equation}
 g^{-2d}F_{r,d}(g\eta)=g^rF_{r,d}(\eta).
\end{equation}
Thus the $F_{r,d}$ are exactly the components of the coefficients
$[g^r]\mathcal W$ computed above, separated by external-field degree:
\begin{align}
 [g^2]\mathcal W(V(g\eta))
 &=F_{2,1}(\eta)+F_{2,0}(\eta),\\
 [g^4]\mathcal W(V(g\eta))
 &=F_{4,1}(\eta)+F_{4,0}(\eta)+F_{4,-1}(\eta).
\end{align}
Coefficient extraction holds $\eta$ fixed.  The first index $r$
records the perturbative order, while the second index $d$
distinguishes the field degrees $r+2d$ within that order:
$4,2$ at order two and $6,4,2$ at order four.

\subsection{Extracting the kinetic coefficient}

The formula produces a quadratic polynomial in $y$.
It is not exactly a multiple of the kinetic term.
%
The marginal coefficient is extracted by testing its bulk density
on affine tangent fields
\begin{equation}
 y_o(o+r)=\mathsf L_o r,\qquad
 \mathsf L_o:\R^2\longrightarrow q_o^\perp,
\end{equation}
where the tangent coordinates are defined in \eqref{eq:canonical-tangent}
and $\mathsf L_o$ is an arbitrary linear map from spatial
displacements to tangent spin vectors. 
The tangent projection is
$\proj_{q_o^\perp}:\R^3\to q_o^\perp$, used in the definition
of $y_o$.
To make this test concrete, choose two tangent vectors
$a_1,a_2\in q_o^\perp$ and write
\begin{equation}
 \mathsf L_o(r_1,r_2)=r_1a_1+r_2a_2,
 \qquad
 \frac12\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o)
 =\frac12\bigl(|a_1|^2+|a_2|^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
The vectors $a_1,a_2$ are the constant increments of the affine
tangent field in the two spatial directions.  This is the meaning
of its slope; $\mathsf L_o$ is not a projection.

\begin{proposition}[Symmetry of the quadratic kinetic correction]
\label{prop:kinetic-symmetry}
Under the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:spin-symmetry}, use
also the equivariant bulk coefficient prescription of the preprint.
The bulk coefficients preserve spatial quarter-turn symmetry.

Let $G_o^{[2]}$ be a homogeneous quadratic output contribution,
summed over the complete symmetry orbits of its labels at anchor
$o$.  Then there is a scalar $\alpha_G$ such that
\begin{equation}
 G_o^{[2]}(\mathsf L_o r)
 =\frac{\alpha_G}{2}\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o)
 =\frac{\alpha_G}{2}\bigl(|a_1|^2+|a_2|^2\bigr).
 \label{eq:kinetic-symmetry}
\end{equation}
Only spatial quarter turns are required; arbitrary spatial rotations
are not symmetries of the lattice construction.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:spin-symmetry}, fixing the anchor spin
leaves an internally $O(2)$-invariant Taylor polynomial.

For spatial quarter turns, the bulk block geometry, free kernels,
and coefficient-assignment rules transform together.  The preprint
keeps complete symmetry orbits of labels, so the summed bulk
quadratic contribution has this symmetry as well.  This statement
concerns the bulk coefficient calculation, not arbitrary winding
terms on a torus without the corresponding spatial symmetry.

Now evaluate $G_o^{[2]}$ on $y_o(o+r)=r_1a_1+r_2a_2$.
The exponential coefficient bounds make this evaluation summable.
Internal $O(2)$ invariance implies that the resulting quadratic form
is a linear combination of dot products:
\begin{equation}
 Q(a_1,a_2)
 =c_1|a_1|^2+2c_{12}a_1\cdot a_2+c_2|a_2|^2.
\end{equation}
A spatial quarter turn exchanges the slopes up to a sign, so
\begin{equation}
 Q(a_1,a_2)=Q(a_2,-a_1)
 =c_1|a_2|^2-2c_{12}a_1\cdot a_2+c_2|a_1|^2.
\end{equation}
Since this holds for arbitrary $a_1,a_2$, it forces
$c_1=c_2$ and $c_{12}=0$.  Setting $\alpha_G=2c_1$ proves
\eqref{eq:kinetic-symmetry}.  An individual unsymmetrized label
need not have this form.
\end{proof}

The symbol $S_{K',o}$ denotes the contribution anchored at $o$ to
the block spin kinetic energy, with the edge and row contributions
assigned to anchors as in the preprint. For small gradients,
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E'(V)=\frac12\langle V,K'V\rangle
              =\sum_o S_{K',o}(V).
\end{equation}
The superscript $[2]$ denotes the homogeneous degree-two Taylor
term in the tangent coordinates $y$:
\begin{equation}
 S_{K',o}^{[2]}(y)
 =\frac12\left.\frac{d^2}{d\varepsilon^2}
                  S_{K',o}(\varepsilon y)\right|_{\varepsilon=0},
 \qquad
 \mathcal E'^{[2]}(y)=\sum_o S_{K',o}^{[2]}(y).
\end{equation}
The full kinetic energy has higher-degree terms because block spins are on the sphere.

The free kinetic term is normalized such that
\begin{equation}
 K'(p)=|p|^2+O(|p|^4)\qquad(p\to0).
\end{equation}
Its quadratic kinetic density therefore has the usual gradient
normalization.  Evaluated on the affine tangent field, it gives
\begin{equation}
 S_{K',o}^{[2]}(\mathsf L_o r)
 =\frac12\sum_{\mu=1}^2|\mathsf L_o e_\mu|^2
 =\frac12\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o).
\end{equation}
We expand \emph{around} a constant block-spin configuration;
the block spins are not kept exactly constant during the test.
At an exactly constant configuration both $\mathcal E'$ and its
kinetic densities vanish.  Their second variation under nonconstant
perturbations determines the kinetic coefficient.  Using the density
$S_{K',o}^{[2]}$ states this normalization per anchor and avoids an
overall volume factor.
%
Define the affine-Hessian functional $\mathfrak a$ by
\begin{equation}
 G_o^{[2]}(\mathsf L_o r)=\mathfrak a(G^{[2]})\,
                    \tfrac12\tr(\mathsf L_o^*\mathsf L_o),
 \qquad \mathfrak a(S_{K'}^{[2]})=1,
\end{equation}
where all bulk labels at the anchor are summed before this test.
Thus the two kinetic increments are
\begin{equation}
 \alpha=\mathfrak a(F_{2,0}),\qquad
 \kappa=\mathfrak a(F_{4,-1}),\qquad
 b^-=b+\alpha+\frac{\kappa}{b}.
 \label{eq:pert-bminus}
\end{equation}
Although $\alpha$ is an order-one change of $b$, its contribution
$\alpha\mathcal E'$ is order $g^2$ when $y=O(g)$.
Similarly $(\kappa/b)\mathcal E'$ is order $g^4$.

The remaining polynomial terms are assigned by the triangular rule
\begin{align}
 P'_4&=F_{2,1},&
 I'_2&=\operatorname{Comp}
             (F_{2,0}-\alpha S_{K'}^{[2]}),\\
 P'_6&=F_{4,1},&
 I'_4&=F_{4,0}-\alpha P'_4-\alpha S_{K'}^{[4]},\\
 J'_2&=\operatorname{Comp}
             (F_{4,-1}-\kappa S_{K'}^{[2]}),&
 P'_5&=I'_3=0.
 \label{eq:pert-slot-recursion}
\end{align}
The operation $\operatorname{Comp}$ writes a quadratic list with
zero total affine Hessian in the compensated $Q_{r,s}$ packets;
it preserves the summed polynomial.  The term $-\alpha S_{K'}^{[4]}$
is needed because shifting $b$ multiplies the whole nonlinear
kinetic energy.  The term $-\alpha P'_4$ is needed because the
new quartic slot is written with prefactor $b^-$ rather than $b$.
Indeed, modulo constants and formal orders above four,
\begin{equation}
 b\mathcal E'+\mathcal W_{\le4}
 =b^-\mathcal E'+b^-P'_4+I'_2+b^-P'_6+I'_4+(b^-)^{-1}J'_2.
\end{equation}
This identity explains both the kinetic recursion and the accompanying
recursion of the irrelevant coefficients. 

\subsection{From the formal recursion to the exact one}

Write $\mathbf P$ for the collection of retained polynomial
coefficient lists,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf P=(P_4,I_2,P_5,I_3,P_6,I_4,J_2),
 \qquad P_5=I_3=0.
\end{equation}
The subscripts indicate degree in the relative tangent field $y$.
The displayed powers of $b$ are not part of these lists: their
contribution to the action is
\begin{equation}
 bP_4+I_2+bP_6+I_4+b^{-1}J_2,
\end{equation}
with the prescribed sums over labels and their masks.  Each entry
of $\mathbf P$ is a coefficient list, not a single scalar coupling.
The coefficient operation defines a deterministic map
$\mathbf P'=\mathcal C_h(\mathbf P)$, independent of the precise
$b$ once its displayed powers have been sorted.  It updates all
these lists together.  Starting from the nearest-neighbor action,
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf P^{(0)}=0,\qquad
 \mathbf P^{(h+1)}=\mathcal C_h(\mathbf P^{(h)}).
\end{equation}
Blocking generates nonzero polynomial terms even from this zero
initial list.
Write $\alpha_h,\kappa_h$ for the increments evaluated on this orbit.
After $h=N-j$ completed steps the perturbative prediction is
\begin{equation}
 b_{j-1}^{\rm pert}=b_j+\alpha_{N-j}+\frac{\kappa_{N-j}}{b_j}.
\end{equation}
The omitted connected terms, cutoffs and localization corrections
produce a new regular remainder and covering terms.  Subtracting
the aligned value of each regular remainder changes the bulk
scalar; subtracting its affine Hessian gives a further kinetic
increment $\Delta_j$.  This scalar is distinct from the square
defect $\Delta(\xi)$ in \eqref{eq:delta}.  The exact construction proves
\begin{equation}
 b_{j-1}=b_j+\alpha_{N-j}+\frac{\kappa_{N-j}}{b_j}+\Delta_j,
 \qquad |\Delta_j|\le C_LH_j^{-1.05}.
 \label{eq:pert-exact-recursion}
\end{equation}
Setting the incoming $f$ to zero does not set this newly generated
$\Delta_j$ to zero.  Discarding it is a perturbative truncation.

Finally, [O3, Section~6.1] proves that the canonical orbit and free
history give convergent increments, with
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_h\longrightarrow-\gamma,
 \qquad \gamma=\frac{\log L}{2\pi},\qquad
 \kappa_h\longrightarrow\kappa_\infty.
\end{equation}
The value of $\alpha_\infty$ is identified there by matching a
finite-volume Laplace coefficient before and after blocking;
$\kappa_\infty$ is not evaluated numerically.  Thus the leading
coarse-graining flow decreases the stiffness by $\gamma$ per step,
and tracing the flow towards the ultraviolet gives the reference
growth $b_j\approx H+\gamma j$, with logarithmic corrections.
